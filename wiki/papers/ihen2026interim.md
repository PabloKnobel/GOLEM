**TL;DR.** The International Human Exposome Network (IHEN), an EU-funded consortium of 12 European institutions, sets out an interim research agenda for the [[exposome]] in Europe ([[ihen2026interim]], p. 10, 12). It has five goals: G1, how global change modifies the exposome; G2, how societal transitions affect it; G3, how it can support public health policy and regulation; G4, how it can improve medicine; and G5, methods, tools and infrastructures (p. 6–7, 11). Its central ask is a long-term European Human Exposome Initiative, with large population-based exposome cohorts and FAIR, federated data systems linked to the European Health Data Space (EHDS), the European Open Science Cloud (EOSC) and ESFRI infrastructures (p. 8, 59–60, 69). This is a policy roadmap with no primary data, so no findings were created.

## Key points
- **What it is.** The roadmap was drafted by Robert Barouki (Inserm) and Roel Vermeulen (Utrecht University). Contributors came from ISGlobal, UU, MU, Inserm, UMCG, IARC, CUT, Oulu, HEAL, ANSES, HERA and AUTH (p. 70).
  - It is an interim version, to be followed by surveys, workshops and policy dialogues that will lead to a final European exposome research agenda (p. 11–12).
  - Each goal is led by an IHEN partner institution (p. 12).
- **Method.** Five goals were set by IHEN partners. Sub-goals came from internal expert consultation and initial stakeholder engagement (p. 12).
  - Priorities were ranked by scientific novelty, population and planetary health relevance, innovation potential and likely policy impact (p. 12).
  - A parallel policy-needs assessment mapped European and national policy frameworks and ran a stakeholder survey. Its gaps were matched against the research gaps (p. 12).
- **Definitions adopted by IHEN** (p. 10):
  - Exposome: "the integrated compilation of all the physical, chemical, biological and psychosocial exposure factors, and their interactions, which have an impact on biology and health."
  - Exposomics, for a science audience: "a transdisciplinary field aimed at a comprehensive and discovery-based understanding of how the exposome influences biology and health."
  - The roadmap also gives an extended IHEN definition and short and extended versions for public communication.
- **Headline figure (secondhand).** Non-genetic drivers are said to account for "over 80%" of the burden of non-communicable disease (p. 6, 10). No source is cited for this figure at those points.
- **G1, global change** (p. 13–25):
  - Climate change (G1.1): joint and cumulative effects of co-occurring hazards on internal exposome markers; how adaptation (e.g. more tree cover, permeable green space) and mitigation reshape exposure profiles (p. 14–15).
  - Biodiversity loss (G1.2): mechanisms via pathogens, pollutant bioaccumulation, the microbiome (biodiversity hypothesis) and food (p. 16–17). It asks for standardized biodiversity metrics (species richness, Shannon/Simpson, functional and genetic diversity, essential biodiversity variables) linked to human exposure data (p. 17). It also asks for ExWAS on biodiversity-related exposures (p. 17) and for research on mental health in high- versus low-biodiversity environments (p. 18).
  - Global pollution (G1.3): non-targeted HRMS screening, PFAS and microplastics, recycled-material contaminants, and combined chemical, physical and psychosocial stressors (p. 20–22).
  - Social inequalities (G1.4): socio-economic position "should not be treated solely as a confounding variable" but as a determinant that structures exposure pathways (p. 23). It also covers life-course epidemiology, embodiment, socio-markers and causal mediation methods (p. 23–24). See [[social-exposome]].
- **G2, transitions** (p. 26–35): energy, mobility (non-exhaust PM from heavier EVs, 30 km/h zones, low-emission zones as natural experiments; p. 28–29), the green and circular economy (p. 30–31), food systems (p. 32–33), and work conditions (remote and platform work, shift work combined with chemical exposure; p. 34–35).
- **G3, policy and regulation** (p. 36–45):
  - Burden of disease and costs (p. 36–37).
  - Urban design, housing and workplaces. Listed urban-exposome aspects include access to green and blue space, proximity to industry, density, segregation, and disadvantaged populations concentrated in hotter neighborhoods (p. 38).
  - Mixture risk assessment and "one substance, one assessment" (p. 40–41).
  - Risk communication: avoid individual blame, and handle trade-offs, e.g. physical activity versus air-pollution exposure (p. 42).
  - Exposome-inspired health promotion, a "positive exposome" (p. 44–45). It asks "what type of green and blue areas … are best for population health (type of trees, grass, ponds, canals)", taking all impacts into account (heat, respiratory health, allergies) (p. 45).
- **G4, medicine** (p. 46–55): precision prevention, genome–exposome integration (it notes that the biobanks used to train precision-medicine models are imbalanced by sex, age, ethnicity and poverty; p. 49), pharmaco-exposomics (heat and air pollution altering drug response; p. 51–52), and disease development and progression, structured as adverse outcome pathways (p. 53–54).
- **G5, methods and infrastructure** (p. 56–66):
  - Wearables and low-cost sensors, and HRMS (p. 56–57).
  - Statistical and machine-learning approaches: ExWAS, causal inference, deep learning (p. 57; see [[multi-exposure-analysis]]).
  - A unified EU exposome database built on IHEN, EIRENE, ELIXIR, BBMRI and PARC with EHDS. It mentions a pan-European cohort, "e.g. a 10 million participant EU cohort" (p. 59–60).
  - AOPs, new approach methodologies (NAMs) and knowledge graphs for causality (p. 60–61); training and doctoral networks (p. 61, 63); and FAIRification with DCAT exposome extensions (p. 65).
- **Cross-cutting priorities** (p. 67–69). There are nine, from "move from fragmented to integrated approaches" to "transform exposome science into European economic and industrial strength" through public–private partnerships and European standards (p. 69).
- **Limitations (Claude's reading).** The roadmap is an interim consultation document with no evidence grading. Most quantitative claims are secondhand, for example 61,000 and 47,000 excess heat-related deaths in Europe in 2022 and 2023 (p. 13, via Gallo et al.). The conclusion refers to "six strategic goals" (p. 67), but the document defines five (p. 6, 11).

## Why it matters for Pablo's work
- Claude's reading, not the authors' claim: this is the agenda his IHEN exposome-ambassador training draws on. The 2026 school's opening joint session covered "the European exposome roadmap" ([[ihen-exposome-training-school-2026]]). Group B, his 2025 cohort, drafted regional roadmaps that week.

G1.4's call to treat SEP as a determinant rather than a confounder matches [[social-exposome]] work such as [[2025-paper-svi-biological-aging-nyc]].
- Aging appears only in passing: older adults are listed among vulnerable groups (p. 21, 29), and working life overlaps with "aging" (p. 34). The roadmap has no sub-goal on aging or the life-course biology of aging.
