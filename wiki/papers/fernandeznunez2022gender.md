**TL;DR.** A systematic review of 16 studies on sex or gender differences in the urban greenness–mental health association found that 7 reported larger benefits for women/females, 3 for men/males, and 4 had no clear beneficiary; only 7 used sex and gender terms correctly and none defined them ([[fernandeznunez2022gender]], p. 3). Safety emerged as a key mediator for women's use of green space (p. 5, 7). Pablo is senior and corresponding author.

## Key points
- Search: 1,157 records, 336 after deduplication, 26 full texts, 9 included plus 7 by snowballing = 16 (p. 2–3, Fig. 1).
- Sex vs gender: sex is the biological dimension (female/male), gender the social one (women/men, power structures) (p. 1).
- Use of terms: 9 studies assessed gender only, 4 both, 3 sex only; all binary; only 7 used terms correctly; none defined them; Currie 2016 was the only one to justify its choice (p. 3).
- Direction by study: secondhand summaries of individual studies (e.g., Koohsari 2018 older women and depression; Bos 2016 strongest at ages 18–24 and >65 in women, adverse for men 45–54) (secondhand via [[fernandeznunez2022gender]], p. 4) → review vote count in [[urban-greenness--mental-health--fernandeznunez2022gender-by-sex-gender]].
- Outcomes: depression (6 studies), stress (5), other disorders (suicide, schizophrenia, mood, OCD, borderline), social health (6) (p. 4–5).
- Safety: reviewers report the included studies were "unanimous" on safety as a mediator, yet over half did not consider safety (p. 7).
- The three studies favoring men were all in Iran (Table 1), where authors cite gender norms limiting women's and girls' use (p. 3–4, 7).
- Recommendations: analyze gender intersectionally with class, race and religion; treat gender as a spectrum; involve women and girls in park design (p. 7–8).
- Authors' limitations: possible missed studies due to varied effect-modification terminology; English only (p. 7).
