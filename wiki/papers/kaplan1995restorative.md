**TL;DR.** Kaplan sets out Attention Restoration Theory: directed attention is effortful, inhibitory and fatigable; restorative environments let it rest through four components (being away, fascination, extent, compatibility), and natural settings are rich in all four. He proposes integrating stress and attention by treating directed-attention depletion as one cause of stress ([[kaplan1995restorative]], p. 169, 172–174, 176–179).

## Key points
- Directed attention: requires effort, is under voluntary control, works through inhibition, and fatigues with prolonged use (p. 170).
- Fatigue shows in perception (distractibility), thought (planning), action (impulsivity) and feeling (irritability) (p. 171–172).
- Four restorative components: being away, fascination (soft fascination allows reflection), extent, compatibility; fascination is necessary but not sufficient (p. 172–173).
- Natural settings score high on all four, even small, nearby ones (p. 174).
- Reviewed studies (Hartig et al. 1991; Cimprich 1992, 1993; Tennessen & Cimprich) show attention gains after nature exposure, mostly without mood changes (p. 175–176). Secondhand: these studies are not yet in the vault.
- Integration: stress arises from harm or resource inadequacy; directed attention is the key depletable resource, so stress and attentional fatigue often co-occur and are confounded in research (p. 176–179).
- Attentional fatigue develops and recovers more slowly than stress (p. 179).

## Why it matters for Pablo's work
- Claude's reading: the mechanism behind "quality" arguments; restorative components (extent, compatibility) are environment attributes that quantity measures such as NDVI do not capture.
- Kaplan's own example of process fascination is people rising early "to catch a glimpse of a small, feathered animal" (p. 172), a link to [[bird-biodiversity]].
