**TL;DR.** A systematic review of 15 multi-dimensional, direct-observation tools for auditing urban green space quality. All showed acceptable inter-rater reliability, but they varied widely in time required, number of items and study-unit definitions, and several quality dimensions (surroundings, usage, land cover, policies, animal and plant biodiversity) were rarely included ([[knobel2019systematic]], p. 1, 4–6). Pablo is first author.

## Key points
- Search: 4,039 records (2,130 WoS, 1,909 Scopus), 58 full texts, 12 tools plus 3 from references (p. 3, Fig. 1).
- Checklist: 27 items in four groups (publication, development, features, dimensions) and 12 quality dimensions: surroundings, accessibility, facilities, amenities, aesthetics and attractions, incivilities, safety, usage/activities, land covers, policies, animal biodiversity, plant biodiversity (p. 2–3, Table 1).
- Dimension coverage across 15 tools: amenities 15, facilities 14, aesthetics 13, accessibility 11, incivilities 11, safety 8, surroundings 4, usage 4, animal biodiversity 3, covers 2, plant biodiversity 2, policies 0 (p. 5, Table 4). Tools covered 3 (CPAT) to 8 (POST, NZ-POST, Veinberga et al.) dimensions (p. 4).
- Training and per-visit time were reported for only four tools: training from half a day (POST) to nine days (PARK); per site from 11 min (NGST) to 67 min (EARPS, 646 items) (p. 3–4, Table 3).
- Tools focused on physical activity until the early 2010s, then broader-focus tools appeared (p. 3).
- Most epidemiologic studies used presence or proximity only; remote sensing cannot detect subtle quality changes, and perceived quality is prone to subjectivity (p. 1).
- Recommendations: clearer shared definitions, more comprehensive dimensions, and reporting of time per visit, technician expertise and inter-rater variability (p. 6).
