**TL;DR.** Of 73 empirical exposome studies that used social variables (from 1,001 records), 55 (~75%) used them as exposures, confounders or both; education and race/ethnicity were the most common individual variables and deprivation indices the most common area-level ones; of 35 hypothesis-driven studies, only 8 discussed a social mechanism ([[neufcourt2022assessing]], p. 1, 3–4). The authors give a decision guide (Figure 3) for using social variables in exposome studies.

## Key points
- Selection: 1,001 records, 536 duplicates, 382 excluded at title/abstract, 10 at full text → 73 (p. 2–3).
- Growth over time: 6 studies before 2017; 16 in 2020; 18 in 2021 (p. 3). 45% Europe, 29% US; 38% around childbirth, 34% adults (p. 3). 26 papers gave no formal definition of the exposome (p. 3).
- 285 social variables: individual 37%, area-level 28%, parental 21%, family/household 14%; 58% used as exposures, ~30% as confounders (p. 3).
- Parental education and participant race/ethnicity were mostly used as confounders (p. 3).
- Of 35 hypothesis-driven studies, 8 discussed a mechanism (behaviors, occupational/domestic exposures, psychosocial stress); 22 (63%) mentioned neither a social hypothesis nor a social-to-biological mechanism (p. 4).
- Argument: agnostic exposome-wide approaches treat "upstream" stratifying variables (race/ethnicity, class) as equivalent to downstream exposures, ignoring their hierarchy (p. 4).
- Interpreting confounder coefficients risks the Table 2 fallacy; some argue social variables should be treated as "fundamental causes" (p. 5).
- Ecological deprivation indices may underestimate social inequality relative to individual indicators (p. 5).
- Recommendation: state the hypothesis and pick among behavioral, material and psychosocial explanatory models (Bartley) before selecting social variables (p. 6, Figure 3).
- Limitations: search on "exposom*" only, English, no grey literature (p. 5).

> [!uncertain] Source inconsistencies
> - Discussion says 12 of 35 (34%) hypothesis-driven papers stated an explicit social hypothesis (p. 4); Results say 22 of 35 (63%) mentioned neither hypothesis nor mechanism, which leaves 13 (p. 4).
