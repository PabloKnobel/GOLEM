**TL;DR.** A short (4-page) narrative review arguing that life-course environmental exposures (air pollution, heavy metals, pesticides and endocrine disruptors, diet, lifestyle) accelerate aging and age-related disease, especially Alzheimer's and Parkinson's, through oxidative stress, inflammation and epigenetic change ([[kareem2025role]], p. S2–S4). No search strategy, no original data; every substantive claim is a one-line summary of a cited source, so all content here is secondhand.

## Key points
- Defines the exposome as "all environmental exposures from conception to death" (abstract, p. S2), spanning air and water pollutants, diet, lifestyle and occupational hazards (p. S2).
- Table 1 maps five exposure groups to aging effects: air pollution (cellular aging, inflammation, CVD and neurodegeneration), heavy metals (mitochondrial dysfunction, oxidative damage), pesticides and endocrine disruptors, diet, and lifestyle (smoking, alcohol, inactivity) (p. S3, Table 1).
- Figure 1 splits the exposome into external (environmental) and internal (lifestyle) factors (p. S3, Fig. 1); this differs from the external/internal split used elsewhere in the vault (see [[exposome]]).
- Mechanisms named: epigenetic modification, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction and chronic inflammation (p. S3).
- Neurodegeneration: air pollution and heavy metals said to raise Alzheimer's risk via amyloid-beta and tau; paraquat and rotenone linked to Parkinson's; gut microbiome dysbiosis proposed as a pathway (secondhand, p. S3–S4).
- Gaps listed: gene–environment interaction, molecular mechanisms and biomarkers, the gut–brain axis, and life-long cohort studies (p. S4).
- Prevention: environmental policy, exposome-based personalized risk profiles, diet and activity interventions (p. S4).
