**TL;DR.** A systematic review (7 studies) and multilevel meta-analysis (6 longitudinal studies, 13 effect sizes) found that each 10 dB increase in long-term residential noise was associated with higher incident Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (pooled HR 1.15, 95% CI 1.03–1.28); differences by noise source and dementia subtype were not significant ([[hwang2025comprehensive]], p. 1, 6–7). Pablo is senior author.

## Key points
- Search of PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science on August 1, 2023: 16,965 records, 9,459 unique, 27 full texts, 7 included; 6 meta-analyzed (one reported dementia mortality) (p. 2–4, Fig. 1).
- Categorical HRs were converted to HR per 10 dB by fitting a line to category medians (p. 3).
- Pooled HR 1.15 (1.03–1.28) per 10 dB → [[environmental-noise--dementia--hwang2025comprehensive]] (p. 6–7).
- Noise source × subtype interaction did not improve fit (QM = 2.38, df = 6, p = 0.88); in the additive model, road and railway noise and vascular dementia had higher point estimates but no subgroup was significant (p. 6).
- Leave-one-out pooled HRs ranged 0.98–1.29; most influential studies were Yuchi et al. and Weuve et al. (p. 6, Fig. 3). No small-study effects (Egger p = 0.132) (p. 7).
- Exposure models ranged from Nord2000 (±3 dB validation) to the FHWA Traffic Noise Model and kriging from daytime samples; common gaps were static traffic data, missing secondary sources and no individual factors such as bedroom placement or insulation (p. 4–5).
- Four studies adjusted for air pollution; three examined joint effects; only one did not adjust for SES (p. 8).
- Proposed mechanisms: stress responses, sleep disruption, inflammation; hearing loss as possible mediator or modifier (p. 2, 8).
- A prior meta-analysis (Meng et al., 2022) mixed mild cognitive impairment and occupational noise; secondhand via this paper (p. 2).
- Authors' limitations: few studies per subgroup, heterogeneous exposure and outcome ascertainment, residual confounding by SES, air pollution and comorbidity (p. 9).
