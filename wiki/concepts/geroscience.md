**Lead.** Geroscience studies how biological aging enables chronic disease, on the premise that aging is the predominant risk factor for most diseases limiting healthspan, and seeks multi-disease prevention by targeting aging itself ([[kennedy2014geroscience]], p. 709). Social scientists argue its biological hallmarks explain only a modest share of variation in human aging outcomes ([[crimmins2020social]], p. 1, 7).

> [!info] Infobox
> **Kind:** research paradigm · **Key frameworks:** seven pillars of aging (Kennedy et al. 2014); nine hallmarks of aging (López-Otín et al. 2013, not in vault) · **Typical measures:** epigenetic clocks, telomere length, mtDNA copy number, composite biological age · **Key sources:** [[kennedy2014geroscience]], [[crimmins2020social]]

## Definition and measurement
- Seven pillars: adaptation to stress, epigenetics, inflammation, macromolecular damage, metabolism, proteostasis, stem cells and regeneration; highly intertwined ([[kennedy2014geroscience]], p. 710–711).
- Crimmins lists López-Otín's nine hallmarks and attributes a seven-pillar set centered on inflammaging to Franceschi et al. (2018) (secondhand via [[crimmins2020social]], p. 1).
- Human-study measures of hallmarks: telomere length, DNA methylation (epigenetic age acceleration), mtDNA copy number; downstream summaries such as biological age, phenotypic age, pace of aging ([[crimmins2020social]], p. 2). Whether methylation clocks capture chronologic or biologic age was open in 2014 ([[kennedy2014geroscience]], p. 712).

## Evidence
- Environmental influences on aging, beyond smoking and obesity, "remain poorly understood" ([[kennedy2014geroscience]], p. 712).
- In HRS data, measured biological aging markers explained 6–10% of variance in aging outcomes → [[biological-aging-measures--age-related-health-outcomes--crimmins2020social]]; social factors explained more → [[social-hallmarks-of-aging--age-related-health-outcomes--crimmins2020social]].
- Neighborhood social vulnerability was linked to faster biological aging measured as [[phenotypic-age]] acceleration in an EHR cohort; authors frame biological aging as modifiable and targetable by policy ([[knobel2025social]], p. 243, 246–247).
