**Lead.** Attention Restoration Theory (ART) holds that directed attention is a fatigable resource and that restorative environments, natural ones above all, let it recover ([[kaplan1995restorative]], p. 169). It is one of the two main psychological pathways proposed between green space and health, alongside stress reduction ([[nieuwenhuijsen2017fifty]], p. 67).

> [!info] Infobox
> **Kind:** theory · **Originators:** S. Kaplan and R. Kaplan · **Key components:** being away, fascination, extent, compatibility · **Key sources:** [[kaplan1995restorative]]

## Definition and measurement
- Directed attention requires effort, is under voluntary control, works through inhibition and is susceptible to fatigue ([[kaplan1995restorative]], p. 170).
- A restorative experience needs four components: being away, fascination ("soft" fascination leaves room for reflection), extent and compatibility. Fascination is necessary but not sufficient ([[kaplan1995restorative]], p. 172–173).
- Measures used in early tests: proofreading performance, Necker Cube Pattern Control, the Symbol Digit Modalities Test and a "perceived restorativeness" score ([[kaplan1995restorative]], p. 175–176).

## Evidence
- Early field and clinical studies showed attentional gains after nature exposure, mostly without mood changes; reviewed by Kaplan, not yet in the vault as primary sources ([[kaplan1995restorative]], p. 175–176).
- Kaplan argues that directed-attention depletion is a cause of stress, so stress and attentional fatigue are often confounded in experiments ([[kaplan1995restorative]], p. 176–179).
- A later review lists restoration and stress reduction as the most consistent pathways linking green space and health ([[nieuwenhuijsen2017fifty]], p. 67).
- Birdsong and natural soundscapes have been linked to attention restoration in experimental studies cited by [[knobel2026residential]] (p. 9–10); these are secondhand.
