## **Untangling 311-Derived Exposures and Their Influence on Frailty Progression Within the Urban Exposome** 

Aging in urban environments reflects the combined influence of social, built, and ambient exposures. While built and ambient conditions are increasingly well-characterized, the social environment remains underdeveloped, limiting our ability to understand how neighborhood conditions affect vulnerability among older adults. New York City’s 311 system provides a high-resolution record of non-emergent resident-reported problems that may reflect objective environmental burdens, population sensitivities, and reporting behaviors, but it remains unclear what these metrics actually capture or how they relate to health among frail individuals. 

The objective of this pilot is to untangle the meaning of 311-based social exposure metrics and evaluate their relevance for frailty progression and frailty-relevant clinical outcomes among older frail adults. Using longitudinal, geocoded electronic health records from the Mount Sinai Health System and the validated Veterans Affairs Frailty Index. **Aim 1** will characterize what 311 metrics represent by analyzing temporal and spatial patterns and comparing complaint themes with groundcondition datasets and neighborhood sociodemographic indicators, distinguishing environmental signals from reporting tendencies. **Aim 2** will quantify associations between annual 311-derived exposures and hospitalization, emergency visits, and mortality among frail older adults using generalized additive mixed models within a longitudinal framework. **Aim 3** will integrate 311-derived social signals with built (e.g., greenness) and ambient (e.g., air pollution, temperature) exposures to generate multidimensional neighborhood exposure profiles using model-based clustering, and evaluate how these cooccurring environmental contexts relate to frailty progression. 

This project introduces a scalable and previously underutilized class of social exposure metrics into aging research, and innovates in its integration of high-resolution social, built, and ambient exposures within a multidomain urban exposome framework. This work will yield publishable findings and provide refined exposure measures, effect-size estimates, and analytic pipelines essential for understanding how urban environments shape frailty trajectories and for informing future research on frailty onset and progression.

## **SPECIFIC AIMS** 

The urban exposome influences healthy aging<sup>1-3</sup> . From a geospatial perspective, it can be conceptualized as the interaction among three core domains—ambient (e.g., air pollution, temperature), built (e.g., greenness, walkability), and social (e.g., neighborhood stressors, community dynamics). While the ambient and built domains are increasingly well-characterized and studied, the social environment remains comparatively underdeveloped despite its critical role in shaping behavioral adaptation, stress exposure, and access to supportive infrastructure<sup>4</sup> . A truly comprehensive urban exposome requires explicit integration of the social environment alongside ambient and built exposures. 

The 311 database of non-emergent service requests represents a promising source of data on neighborhood social conditions<sup>5</sup> . Each record includes precise spatial coordinates and timestamps, enabling the generation of social exposure metrics with high spatial and temporal resolution<sup>5</sup> . The volume, continuity, and diversity of 311 complaints across categories—such as noise, sanitation, housing, or street conditions—offer an opportunity to characterize the lived social environment with a granularity complementing the commonly used census-based indicators<sup>6</sup> . 

Frailty, a multidimensional indicator of age-related physiological vulnerability, affects a large and growing proportion of the U.S. older adult population and predicts hospitalization, disability, and mortality<sup>7</sup> . Studying frailty progression, rather than prevalence alone, allows us to capture dynamic changes in physiological resilience<sup>8</sup> and how these changes evolve in response to the social, built, and ambient exposures that define the urban exposome. 

The overall goal of this pilot is to untangle what 311-based social exposure metrics capture and assess how they relate to frailty development and progression—both independently and as part of integrated urban exposome profiles. The study will be led by Drs. Knobel and Yitshak Sade (MPIs), environmental epidemiologists specializing in urban exposomics and electronic health records (EHR) analysis, in collaboration with Drs. Kloog (geospatial modeling), Midya (biostatistics), and Ko (aging and frailty). The study leverages the rich administrative and clinical data resources available at Mount Sinai Health System (MSHS) and the interdisciplinary expertise within the P30 Center. Our specific aims are: 

**Aim 1. Untangle exposure metrics from 311 service requests.** We will assess data quality and compare it with alternative ground-condition datasets. Additionally, we will examine neighborhood sociodemographic indicators to evaluate whether 311 complaints reflect actual environmental conditions or reflect differential reporting behavior. 

**Aim 2. Evaluate associations between 311 exposures and secondary clinical outcomes related to frailty.** We will leverage the MSHS EHR, which includes longitudinal, geocoded residential data for patients residing in New York City (2011–2023), as well as the Veterans Affairs Frailty Index (VA-FI)<sup>9</sup> , to identify the frail population. We will assess associations between time-varying 311 exposures and frailty-relevant clinical outcomes, including falls, hip fractures, pressure ulcers, all-cause mortality, and healthcare utilization (hospitalizations, emergency visits, or institutionalization). We will use generalized additive models for single exposures and quantile G- computation to model the associations of the mixture theme-specific 311 complaints. 

**Aim 3. Integrate 311 complaints with ambient and built exposures to identify neighborhood exposure profiles and assess their association with frailty progression.** We will integrate 311-based social environment metrics with built (e.g., greenness) and ambient (e.g., air pollution) exposures to generate exposure profile clusters using model-based clustering. These clusters will represent multidimensional neighborhood exposure profiles across NYC. We will then assess associations between these exposure profiles and frailty progression using a growth mixture model to construct frailty trajectory groups<sup>10</sup> and generalized additive models. 

**Impact:** This pilot will **(1)** untangle what 311-based social exposure metrics represent, distinguishing environmental signals from reporting patterns; **(2)** quantify their associations with frailty-relevant clinical outcomes; and **(3)** construct multidimensional neighborhood exposure profiles that integrate social, built, and ambient domains. Further, **(4)** it will generate preliminary results to support Dr. Knobel’s career development award.

## **BACKGROUND & SIGNIFICANCE:** 

Aging is shaped by complex, intertwined environmental influences<sup>1-3</sup> . The exposome—the integrated compilation of all physical, chemical, biological, and psychosocial factors and their interactions that impact biology and health—offers a comprehensive framework for understanding how environmental factors contribute to human aging<sup>11</sup> . The urban exposome can be understood as a three-legged stool—ambient (e.g., air pollution, temperature), built (e.g., greenness, walkability), and social (e.g., day-to-day neighborhood conditions and behaviors). While ambient and built domains are increasingly data-rich and standardized, the social environment remains the weakest link<sup>4</sup> , limiting our ability to capture how neighborhoods influence aging trajectories. 

The 311 databases provide access to non-emergency city services across the U.S. Each complaint includes precise coordinates and timestamps, offering a scalable, high-resolution window into neighborhood social conditions<sup>12</sup> . 311 complaints could encode three distinct social signals that standard census indicators might miss<sup>5</sup> : (A) Objective neighborhood conditions (e.g., noise, sanitation, housing problems), (B) Population sensitivity/perception of similar conditions, and (C) Propensity/ability to complain (engagement, advocacy, or trust). Treating 311 complaints solely as objective issues discards B and C, two critical factors which influence how environmental exposures might shape health<sup>6</sup> . 

As a multidimensional construct of physiological vulnerability, frailty captures the cumulative impact of intrinsic and extrinsic stressors, making it relevant for assessing environmental exposures that may accelerate or mitigate age-related decline<sup>13</sup> . Frailty is marked by heightened susceptibility to adverse events like mortality, morbidity, disability, and hospitalizations<sup>7</sup> . Studying frailty progression, rather than prevalence, allows us to capture dynamic changes in physiological resilience<sup>8</sup> and how these evolve in response to the social, built, and ambient exposures that define the urban exposome. Leveraging Mount Sinai’s longitudinal Electronic Health Records (EHR), we will align time-varying 311 exposures with annual Veterans Affairs Frailty Index (VA-FI) measures, test overall and complaint type-specific associations, and embed social metrics within ambient/built mixtures to explain frailty development. 

## **INNOVATION:** 

Conceptually, this project elevates the social environment to a first-class component of the urban exposome and clarifies what information 311 service-request data truly capture. Methodologically, it leverages geotagged 311 data to construct census-tract–level social exposure metrics that distinguish objective and subjective signals and capture both overall and complaint-type-specific patterns. Analytically, it models longitudinal frailty progression using the VA-FI with time-aligned, tract-level exposures and incorporates both complaint-mixture analysis and neighborhood exposure profiles spanning social, built, and ambient domains. This pilot will also reduce key uncertainties needed to support a competitive grant by identifying which 311 metrics carry meaningful signal and establishing preliminary effect-size ranges for frailty progression, directly informing exposure selection, modeling strategy, and power calculations. 

## **APPROACH** 

## **Aim 1. Untangle exposure metrics from 311 service requests.** 

<u>Metric procurement: We will extract, clean, and standardize all 311 complaints for New York City from 2011 to</u> 2023 obtained through NYC Open Data (Open NYC)<sup>14</sup> , map them to census tract levels, and generate yearly complaint rates per population and per area. Metrics will include overall complaint rates and theme-specific complaint rates (e.g., noise, sanitation, housing). Our team has already produced overall yearly census-tract 311 rates and successfully linked cohort geocodes to other urban exposures. 

<u>Metric untangling: First, we will assess data quality through missingness summaries, spatial outlier detection,</u> and temporal and spatial autocorrelation checks. Second, we will compare 311-derived exposures with alternative ground-condition data—for example, the Primary Land Use Tax Lot Output (PLUTO) dataset or road and rail network data—to evaluate whether complaints reflect actual environmental conditions or reflect differential reporting behavior. Finally, we will use Moran’s I, a bivariate measure of spatial autocorrelation<sup>15</sup> to assess the spatial association between 311 metrics and neighborhood sociodemographic indicators derived from the census, to determine whether any reporting differences are driven by sociodemographic composition. 



<!-- page 5 -->

**Aim 2. Evaluate associations between 311 exposures and secondary clinical outcomes related to frailty.** <u>Study population:</u> We will leverage 12 years of electronic health records from the MSHS, serving ~3 million people annually and including records for over 12 million patients of diverse backgrounds and healthcare needs. The study population is representative of older adults in NYC, suggesting that our findings will have broader applicability. For our analysis, we will include only frail patients as defined by the Veterans Affairs Frailty Index (VA-FI). Frailty will be defined as VA-FI ≥ 0.21<sup>9</sup> . We will exclude participants with no valid residential address or address outside NYC (~10%). We will follow participants from their first documented MSHS encounter with frailty until death, loss, or the end of follow-up. If two consecutive years show no indication of MSHS usage, the last year of service usage indicated will be considered the last year of follow-up. Our previous analysis in this cohort shows that missing data and errors due to low EHR data continuity are minimal with this approach<sup>16</sup> . 

<u>Frailty assessment: We will determine each person’s frailty state using the VA-FI tool</u><sup>9</sup> . VA-FI uses the cumulative deficit method outlined by Searle et al.<sup>17</sup> . This validated approach uses EHR data and aligns with guidelines for frailty index construction in aging research<sup>63</sup> . Variables included in the index meet four criteria<sup>38</sup> : (a) association with health status, (b) increasing prevalence with age, (c) no saturation before age 65, and (d) coverage of diverse physiological systems. A total of 31 deficits will be drawn from diagnostic and procedure codes. Each variable will be assessed annually using a 3-year lookback window (the year of assessment and two prior years). If a relevant deficit is absent during that window, the corresponding deficit will be coded as absent. The VA-FI will be calculated by dividing the number of observed deficits by the total number of possible deficits, producing a continuous score from 0 to 1. If unexpected data-structure differences arise between the health system in which the VA-FI was developed and ours, we will create an ad hoc FI, as per Searle et al.17. Several studies have effectively used similar methodologies to build novel frailty measures<sup>18</sup> , including Dr. Ko’s<sup>19</sup> . <u>Exposure assessment: Yearly 311 exposure metrics will be assigned based on each participant’s census tract,</u> based on their residential geocode, with residential history updated at each participant visit to account for address changes. Geocodes have already been obtained and validated by Drs. Knobel and Yitshak-Sade through prior linkage to other urban exposure datasets<sup>16,20,21</sup> . 

<u>Outcome assessment: We will derive frailty-relevant secondary outcomes from electronic health records—</u> including falls, hip fractures, pressure ulcers, all-cause mortality, and healthcare utilization (hospitalizations, emergency visits, or institutionalization). Drs. Knobel and Yitshak Sade have previously generated similar outcomes using the same data source<sup>16,20,21</sup> . 

<u>Covariates: We will extract a rich set of covariates from each participant’s EHR. Demographic variables will</u> include age, race and ethnicity, sex, and type of insurance (as a proxy for socioeconomic status). We will construct Directed Acyclic Graphs (DAGs) to guide covariate selection at both individual and neighborhood levels. To address time-varying confounding, we will update covariates annually, incorporate spline terms for calendar year to restrict residual temporal confounding, and explore stabilized inverse probability weights in sensitivity analyses. 

<u>Data Analysis Plan</u> – Single exposures: We will use mixed-effects models to estimate single-exposure associations between overall and theme-specific 311 complaint rates and frailty-relevant secondary outcomes. Single-exposure analyses are essential for novel exposures, as they establish baseline interpretability before moving to mixture-based analyses. Given the longitudinal design and repeated person-year observations, we will employ an Anderson–Gill approach. We will model mortality with a Cox proportional hazards model and hospitalization and emergency visits with Poisson models, all implemented within a generalized additive mixed model (GAMM) framework. This framework allows flexible adjustment for time trends and non-linear exposure– response functions using penalized cubic splines. Random intercepts will be included at both the individual and census tract levels to account for within-person and spatial clustering. We will apply false discovery rate (FDR) correction to adjust for multiple comparisons across exposure types. 

<u>Data Analysis Plan</u> – Mixture: We will use quantile q-computation (qGcomp) to investigate the association between the complete mixture of 311-themes and frailty-relevant secondary health outcomes. This approach is well-suited to mixture analyses involving highly correlated exposures with heterogeneous directional effects. The 



<!-- page 6 -->

overall mixture effect will be interpreted as the expected change in risk associated with simultaneously increasing all exposures in the mixture by one quantile<sup>22</sup> . We will report overall mixture estimates, direction-specific estimates, individual exposure weights (with directionality), and corresponding confidence intervals. These weights will highlight which exposures contribute most to the observed association and in which direction. The study team has previously published using qGcomp to examing mixtures of social exposures<sup>20</sup> . 

## **Aim 3. Integrate 311 with ambient and built exposures to identify neighborhood exposure profiles and assess their association with frailty progression.** 

<u>Exposure profile creation: We will integrate 311-based social metrics with built and ambient exposures (e.g.,</u> urban nature, air pollution, temperature) to derive multidimensional neighborhood exposure profiles. Exposures will be standardized and, if needed, summarized using principal components analysis. We will then apply modelbased clustering (Gaussian mixture models) to identify distinct neighborhood profiles, selecting the optimal number of clusters and covariance structure using the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). 

<u>Frailty trajectory: Frailty trajectories will be obtained using Growth Mixture Models (GMM). GMM is a flexible</u> longitudinal modeling framework that identifies distinct subgroups of individuals who follow different frailty trajectories over time, while also allowing for person-level variation within each subgroup<sup>23</sup> . GMM estimates an intercept, slope, and growth factor variances for each latent class, assigns individuals to classes based on posterior probabilities<sup>24</sup> , and can incorporate covariates that influence frailty progression<sup>25</sup> . The approach is wellsuited for heterogeneous frailty patterns and can accommodate missing data, correlated residuals, and complex covariance structures<sup>24</sup> . 

<u>Data analysis plan:</u> Exposure profiles will be modeled as categorical predictors to test whether distinct cooccurring environmental context types differentially influence frailty progression. We will use generalized additive mixed models (GAMMs) with random intercepts at both the individual and census-tract levels to account for within-person and spatial clustering, consistent with the modeling approach used in Aim 2. The research team has previously applied similar clustering methods to derive urban-nature exposure profiles for this same population<sup>26</sup> . 

## **Potential Challenges and Mitigation Strategies:** 

**(A)** Individuals may move between neighborhoods in ways correlated with their underlying health status, potentially biasing exposure estimates if not accounted for. To address this, we will update the residential address at each participant visit and conduct sensitivity analyses restricted to individuals with a stable residential history. Additionally, we will explore lagged exposure windows to reduce simultaneity between environmental conditions and subsequent health outcomes. **(B)** 311 complaints may not thoroughly or uniformly represent underlying neighborhood social conditions, particularly if reporting behaviors differ across communities. However, Aim 1 is explicitly designed to characterize what 311-based metrics capture by comparing them with both ground-condition and sociodemographic indicators. This will allow us to clarify whether 311 reflects environmental burden, reporting sensitivity, or civic engagement — strengthening interpretability rather than treating this uncertainty as a limitation. However, if Aim 1 results suggest that 311 complaints are not suitable for use as an exposure, the proposed pipeline for Aims 2 and 3 can readily be adapted to use CDC’s Environmental Justice Index, which is already publicly available at the census-tract level. 

## **FUTURE FUNDING** 

This pilot will produce both publishable findings and the methodological groundwork required for a robust career development award from Dr. Knobel or an R01 focused on environmental and social determinants of frailty. Building on the innovative elements described above, the project will **(1)** determine which 311-derived social exposure metrics reflect meaningful neighborhood conditions versus reporting behavior, **(2)** estimate preliminary effect-size ranges for associations with frailty-related clinical outcomes, and **(3)** develop multidimensional neighborhood exposure profiles integrating social, built, and ambient domains. These products—validated exposure measures, effect-size benchmarks, and reproducible analytic pipelines—will substantially reduce uncertainty in exposure selection, modeling strategy, and power calculations for a larger grant examining how urban environmental contexts shape frailty onset and progression. This work directly aligns with the National Institute of Aging’s priorities on social determinants, resilience, and environmental contributors to aging.

## **A. Personal Statement** 

I am an Environmental Epidemiologist in the Department of Environmental Medicine at the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai, and my research examines how multidomain urban exposures influence cardiometabolic and aging-related outcomes. My work encompases on the full analytic arc of environmental epidemiology—developing and geoprocessing novel exposure datasets, leveraging existing administrative and clinical cohorts, and applying mixture-based epidemiologic methods to understand how interacting environmental conditions shape human health 

A substantial component of my work focuses on integrating high-resolution environmental exposures with largescale electronic health record (EHR) data. For example, in a New York City EHR-based study, I investigated how different dimensions of social vulnerability relate to biological aging, demonstrating that social conditions exert distinct and measurable impacts on aging trajectories. I have also led analyses using comprehensive Mount Sinai EHR cohorts to evaluate the combined effects of multiple environmental exposures on cardiometabolic outcomes. 

My research program also extends to national-level administrative cohorts. I have worked with U.S. Medicare data to examine how urban environmental exposures influence morbidity, hospitalizations, and mortality risk among older adults. This work complements my EHR and field-based projects by providing populationrepresentative evidence on how environmental mixtures shape aging outcomes at scale. 

Beyond EHR-based studies, I lead and collaborate on urban exposome projects using diverse population datasets. Through the P30 HEALS pilot program, I’m currently leading the Tweets to Thoughts project, linking bird biodiversity indicators to cognitive health in older adults across New York City—one of the first studies to integrate community-generated ecological signals with aging outcomes. I’m also conducting a GECC-funded

## **A. Personal Statement** 

I am an environmental epidemiologist and Associate Professor in the Department of Environmental Medicine at the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai. My research interests revolve around exploring the complex relationship between various environmental and built environment exposures and their impact on aging-related outcomes, including cognitive decline, dementia, frailty, and cardiometabolic disease. I use rigorous epidemiologic methods and advanced statistical modeling to study these relationships across large, diverse populations, with expertise in the use of electronic health records (EHRs) and linked environmental data. 

I lead and collaborate on large-scale epidemiologic studies using U.S. nationwide Medicare data, EHRs, and registry-based cohorts to examine the effects of the environment on morbidity, hospital admissions, and mortality risk among millions of people. The key focus of my research is disentangling complex exposure mixtures and identifying especially harmful chemical components, which can inform both public health interventions and policy. For example, I was awarded NIEHS Outstanding New Environmental Scientist ONES (R01 ES034864-01) and KL2 (KL2TR004421) awards to investigate the association of World Trade Center (WTC) related exposure and subsequent environmental exposures with cardiometabolic health among responders. This work has already resulted in three publications showing increased cardiometabolic risk associated with WTC-related exposure and subsequent air pollution exposure. We showed that WTC-related exposures are associated with worsening trajectories of systolic blood pressure and glucose among responders. These findings carry significant weight, as scientific evidence plays a pivotal role in assessing whether health conditions stemming from the 9/11 terrorist attack warrant coverage for responders. 

I also led a national study of over 30 million Medicare beneficiaries investigating how specific fine particulate matter (PM2.5) components contribute to cardiovascular disease mortality, which was recently published in _NEJM Evidence_ . In another project, I am investigating the associations between a mixture of environmental exposures and stroke in a national study in Israel (U.S – Israeli binational science foundation (BSF), grant 2021197). Finally, I was recently awarded a P30 pilot grant to investigate Environmental risk factors for Alzheimer’s disease and related dementia (ADRD) among Veterans across the United States. 

In the past four years, I have been mentoring Dr. Knobel on different projects investigating the associations between urban exposure, cardiometabolic health, and aging. ## **A. Personal Statement** 

I am an Associate Professor of Geriatrics and Palliative Medicine at Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai (ISMMS) and Associate Director of Clinical Innovation, Geriatric Research Education and Clinical Center (GRECC) at James J Peters VA Medical Center (JJPVA). I utilize dual translational and clinical gerontology research approaches to identify aging-related factors that increase vulnerability in individuals who are frail and develop clinical interventions to mitigate frailty-associated poor outcomes. My recent clinical research studies include: **(1)** PCORI/NIA-funded STRIDE Study – a multisite cluster randomized pragmatic clinical trial to determine the effectiveness of an evidence-based, multifactorial, individually-tailored intervention to reduce the risk of serious fall injuries among non-institutionalized older persons; **(2)** CDC/NIOSH-funded U01 studies – cross sectional and prospective studies to develop a frailty index, assess frailty trajectories, and implement frailty mitigating multicomponent interventions in World Trade Center general responders; and **(3)** several foundation and NIA-funded biomarker studies examining predictors of adverse post-surgery or post-hospitalization outcomes in frail older adults. I am experienced in the development, implementation and evaluation of new models of care delivery and have led numerous clinical demonstration projects at ISMMS and JJPVA including: **(1)** a population health-based approach to deliver palliative care to Veterans with high Care Assessment Needs scores; **(2)** a pre-operative geriatric-surgery-social work co-management program that optimizes frail older patients undergoing elective surgeries; **(3)** pre-operative frailty assessment and high-risk interdisciplinary surgery board consultation (Surgical Pause) in older Veterans undergoing surgeries; and **(4)** an interdisciplinary geriatric-HIV program, facilitated by community health workers, that delivers age-friendly care to older adults living with HIV.
