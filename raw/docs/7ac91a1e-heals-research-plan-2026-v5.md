**1. Specific aims and hypotheses**

Where older adults live shapes how they age, but nearby nature can only help those who can reach it and perceive it. Living near nature is consistently associated with lower mortality and better function in later life.<sup>1-3</sup> Most proposed pathways, however, depend on contact: physical activity, social engagement, and restoration through seeing and hearing nature.<sup>4</sup> Impairment can block contact in two ways. Mobility and cognitive impairment keep people at home: about 2 million older Americans are homebound.<sup>5</sup> Vision and hearing impairment may let people go out but keep them from perceiving what surrounds them, such as plants they cannot see or birds they cannot hear.<sup>6</sup>

Knowing who benefits from nearby nature matters, because nature is increasingly promoted as a healthy-aging strategy while impairment is common. However, three gaps remain. **(1)** Impairment is rarely tested as a modifier. Reviews call for stratifying by mobility limitation and disability.<sup>7,8</sup> The closest studies used a single global disability measure in a Chinese long-term care cohort<sup>9</sup> or stratified by disability in daily activities.<sup>10</sup> No study we found has tested sensory, motor, and cognitive impairment separately, or in a nationally representative US sample, leaving unclear which older adults miss out on nature’s benefits. **(2)** Exposure is assigned, not used. Studies assign nature to people by their home address, which assumes they go out and use it. Mobility limitation is a main reason they may not: in the National Health and Aging Trends Study (NHATS), physical limitations and probable dementia were associated with going outside less often.<sup>11</sup> Whether nearby nature changes how often older adults with impaired mobility go out has not been tested. **(3)** Most studies rely on the normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI), a satellite measure of vegetation that cannot tell whether nature is accessible or usable,<sup>12</sup> leaving planners without evidence on which features of nearby nature, including access, matter for people with impairment.

This proposal links NHATS (2011–2025), a nationally representative panel of Medicare beneficiaries aged 65 and older, to three census-tract measures of nearby nature: greenness (NDVI), park access, and park presence and quality. NHATS suits this question because it measures sensory, motor, and cognitive impairment, records going out and participation every year, and follows participants to death. Guided by the environmental press–competence model, both aims adjudicate between two competing hypotheses: that impaired adults benefit more from nearby nature (environmental docility) or less (out of reach).

**Aim 1. Test whether impairment modifies the association between nearby nature and out-of-home behavior.** We will relate each nature measure to how often participants go out (primary outcome), with impairment measured in the previous round. Modifiers are vision, hearing, mobility (Short Physical Performance Battery, SPPB), and cognition (NHATS dementia classification).

**Aim 2. Test whether impairment modifies the association between nearby nature and all-cause mortality.** Using the same exposures and modifiers, we will estimate whether the association between nearby nature and survival differs by impairment domain.

**Impact.** This pilot will show whether impaired older adults miss out on the benefits attributed to nearby nature, and whether the barrier is reaching it. For policymakers, it will indicate which features of nearby nature, such as parks within a short walk, to prioritize for an aging population. The results will also set the effect sizes and design for an NIA (National Institute on Aging) R01 that collects primary data from older adults with impairment.

**2. Background and significance**

**Nearby nature is linked to longer, healthier aging.** A meta-analysis of nine cohorts with more than 8 million people found 4% lower mortality per 0.1 increase in NDVI (hazard ratio \[HR\] 0.96; 95% confidence interval \[CI\]: 0.94, 0.97), with high heterogeneity.<sup>2</sup> In older adults, greenness has been associated with lower mortality,<sup>13</sup> less disability in activities of daily living (ADL) and instrumental activities of daily living (IADL),<sup>3</sup> and slower cognitive decline,<sup>14</sup> although evidence for dementia is mixed.<sup>15</sup>

**Most benefits require contact, but exposure is assigned by address.** Nature is thought to act by reducing harm (air pollution, heat, noise), restoring capacities, and building capacities.<sup>4</sup> Only the first works without contact. In the Nurses’ Health Study, depression and social engagement together explained about half of the greenness–mortality association, while fine particulate matter (PM2.5) explained about 4%.<sup>1</sup> Because harm reduction does not require contact, it should not depend on whether impaired adults can reach nature; this pilot therefore focuses on contact pathways. Yet most studies, including this one, assign nature to each person with geographic information systems (GIS), by measuring vegetation or parks around the home address. This measures what is nearby, not what is used. Going out is itself prognostic: older adults who left home daily survived longer than those who rarely did, independent of function.<sup>16</sup> In other words, residential exposure assumes a contact that impairment may prevent.

**Impairment limits both reaching and perceiving nature.** In NHATS, 18.9% of community-dwelling older adults report functional hearing impairment only, 5.4% vision impairment only, and 3.1% both.<sup>17</sup> About 2 million older Americans are homebound,<sup>5</sup> and among those who become homebound, 28.6% have probable dementia and 77% an ADL difficulty.<sup>18</sup> Physical limitations and probable dementia are associated with going outside less often,<sup>11</sup> and hearing and dual sensory impairment with lower odds of leaving home.<sup>19</sup> Impairment may also limit benefit among those who do go out. Restoration depends on perceiving nature, by seeing vegetation and hearing natural sounds. Hearing loss reduces recognition of natural sounds,<sup>6</sup> and age-related hearing loss reduces the ability to detect and distinguish birdsong.<sup>20</sup> An older adult with vision or hearing loss may walk through a park yet miss much of what makes it restorative.

**The press–competence model predicts two competing effects.** Lawton and Nahemow<sup>21</sup> proposed that behavior and wellbeing depend on the fit between a person’s competence and environmental press, the demands an environment places on that person. The model holds that the lower a person’s competence, the more the environment shapes their behavior (the environmental docility hypothesis).<sup>22</sup> Competence spans several domains; we focus on the three that NHATS measures: sensory-perceptual (vision, hearing), motor (mobility), and cognitive. Applied to nature, the model yields two competing hypotheses. Under *environmental docility*, nearby nature matters more for impaired adults, because their environment shapes more of their behavior and health. Under *out of reach*, nearby nature matters less, because impaired adults cannot reach it (motor, cognitive) or perceive it (sensory-perceptual). Figure 1 shows where each domain may intervene; Aims 1 and 2 adjudicate between the two hypotheses.

**3. Innovation**

This proposal offers four innovations. **First, impairment as a modifier, grounded in theory.** Greenspace studies typically stratify by age, sex, or socioeconomic status, and treat impairment as an outcome. We apply the press–competence model to environmental epidemiology and pre-specify competing hypotheses, so either result is informative. **Second, reach as the barrier.** Prior work treats impairment as one barrier and assumes residents use the nature near their homes. By modeling sensory, motor, and cognitive domains separately, and linking nearby nature to both leaving home (Aim 1) and survival (Aim 2), we test whether impairment keeps people from reaching nature. **Third, objective, domain-specific measures.** We use performance tests, audiometry, vision tests, and the NHATS dementia classification rather than a single self-reported disability item. **Fourth, usable nature in a national sample.** We pair NDVI with walkable park access and a national layer of park features, such as walking paths and playgrounds, mapped from aerial imagery with deep learning,{Knobel, 2026} building on the PI’s work on green space quality.<sup>23-25</sup> To our knowledge, this is the first test of these questions in a national sample of urban-dwelling US older adults.

**4. Research design and approach**

**Study population.** NHATS is a nationally representative panel of Medicare beneficiaries aged 65 and older, interviewed in person every year since 2011. The 2011 cohort enrolled 8,245 participants,<sup>26</sup> and new samples were added in 2015, 2022, and 2023 to keep the panel nationally representative. NHATS oversamples the oldest old and Black beneficiaries. We will include community-dwelling participants living in census tracts within Census urban areas, in Rounds 1–14 (2011 to early 2025) and exclude Round 10 (2020), which was conducted by phone during COVID-19 and has no physical performance tests.

**Exposures.** We will build all exposures with GREENER, Mount Sinai’s geospatial data science platform, which curates environmental data, including Landsat NDVI, and harmonizes it to US census geographies (supported by CTSA grant UL1TR004419). For each year from 2011 to 2024, we will compute three tract-level measures and import them into the NHATS secure data enclave through an External File Linkage Request. Each round is linked to the tract of residence in that round, so exposure updates when a participant moves.

- *Greenness:* annual NDVI from Landsat (30 m resolution), averaged within each census tract.
- *Park access:* share of tract residents living within a 10-minute walk (about half a mile, along the street network) of a park.
- *Park presence and quality:* whether the tract contains a park (ParkServe polygons) and, if so, the quality of its parks.

**Modifiers:**

- *Sensory-perceptual:* vision and hearing. Self-reported functional impairment is available in all rounds: vision, blindness or inability to recognize someone across the street or read newspaper print; hearing, deafness, hearing-aid use, or inability to use the telephone or follow a conversation with a television or radio on (with glasses or hearing aids if used). Because hearing-aid use counts as impairment under this definition, a sensitivity analysis will drop that criterion. Objective distance vision and pure-tone audiometry (Rounds 11–14) will be used in a sensitivity analysis.
- *Motor:* original SPPB score (0–12)<sup>27</sup> from balance, gait speed, and chair stands, as scored in NHATS, analyzed as continuous.
- *Cognitive:* NHATS dementia classification (probable vs. no dementia, excluding possible dementia; sensitivity analyses group possible dementia with no dementia or with probable dementia), which combines reported diagnosis, an informant screening interview, and cognitive tests.<sup>28</sup>

**Outcomes.**

- *Aim 1:* going-out frequency in the last month (every day to never; primary). Secondary: walking for exercise in the last month (yes or no) and the number of other valued activities attended (visiting friends or family, religious services, clubs, going out for enjoyment).
- *Aim 2:* all-cause mortality, using month and year of death from the NHATS Sensitive files.

**Covariates.** We will select covariates with a directed acyclic graph (DAG): age, sex, race and ethnicity, education, income, Medicaid enrollment, living arrangement, multimorbidity, round, season, and tract context.

**Data access and fallback.** Tract-level linkage and death dates require NHATS restricted data, which need a data use agreement and IRB approval. We will submit to the Mount Sinai IRB as soon as the award is noticed, expecting an exempt determination, and prepare the NHATS restricted-data request while the IRB is reviewed. Meanwhile, we will build all outcome, modifier, and covariate variables in the public files. If restricted access is delayed, public-file neighborhood items (see Preliminary data) can stand in.

**Preliminary data.** Two analyses of the NHATS public files (Rounds 1–14 except Round 10) show that the data support the planned models.

*<u>Impairment and going out</u>.* The analytic panel includes 13,712 community-dwelling participants in metropolitan counties, with 57,262 person-rounds and 3,378 deaths. Each impairment domain is common enough to model (**Table 1**). After adjustment for age, sex, race and ethnicity, education, and cohort, impaired vision, low mobility, and probable dementia were associated with 50% to 70% lower odds of going out daily, whereas hearing impairment was associated with only 12% lower odds, suggesting that hearing loss alone rarely keeps older adults at home.

*Neighborhood cohesion, impairment, and walking.* The public files contain no nature measures, so we used perceived neighborhood social cohesion to test the Aim 1 model structure. Like nearby nature, cohesion is a neighborhood resource that may draw older adults out of the home, but it is self-reported and may partly reflect how often people go out, so this analysis tests feasibility, not the hypotheses. With walking for exercise as the outcome and impairment from the previous round, cohesion was associated with more walking among unimpaired adults (odds ratio 1.22 to 1.26 per SD) and less so among adults with vision impairment (ratio of odds ratios 0.86, p = .007) or hearing impairment (0.91, p = .010), both significant after FDR correction across the four domains; interactions for mobility (0.96, p = .355) and dementia (0.99, p = .848) were not significant (Figure 2). The planned models run on NHATS data and can detect interactions of this size.

**Potential challenges and alternative approaches.**

***(1)** Tract-level exposure.* Tract averages do not capture each person’s exposure. This error is mostly Berkson-type, which mainly reduces precision rather than biasing estimates. We will compare population-weighted tract means, which weight the parts of a tract where people live, with simple area-weighted means. Impaired adults may stay closer to home, so the tract may describe their exposure better than it does for unimpaired adults; this could make associations look stronger among impaired adults and favor docility, which we will consider when interpreting interactions. ***(2)** Reverse causation and selective moves.* Impairment may prompt a move to a different neighborhood. Modifiers are lagged by one round, and a sensitivity analysis excludes person-rounds after a move. ***(3)** Residential self-selection.* Few participants move, so nature varies mainly between people, and those who value the outdoors may choose greener neighborhoods. Covariates address measured factors, and E-values gauge unmeasured confounding. ***(4)** Attrition and proxy interviews.* Impaired participants are more often lost to follow-up or interviewed by proxy, which could attenuate interactions. We will use inverse probability of attrition weights, adjust for proxy status, report the share of proxy interviews by impairment domain, and repeat the analyses excluding proxy interviews. ***(5)** Mortality ascertainment.* Sensitive-file death dates under-ascertain deaths compared with Medicare records. Following Gill et al,<sup>29</sup> we will censor follow-up at the last completed interview. ***(6)** Enclave output rules.* No results below Census Division may leave the enclave. Our estimates are national model coefficients, so this rule does not limit the aims, but tract-level maps of NHATS participants cannot be shown.

**5. Data analysis**

**Aim 1.** We will fit survey-weighted generalized estimating equations (GEE), which account for repeated interviews of the same person, using NHATS analytic weights and design variables. Each person-round carries that round’s analytic weight and design variables, so pooled estimates represent the population of each round. Going-out frequency is ordered (every day to never), so we will use a cumulative-logit model; the number of activities is a count, so we will use a Poisson model with robust standard errors; walking for exercise is binary, so we will use a logistic model. Each model includes one nature measure, one impairment domain from the previous round, and their interaction. The interaction is the primary estimate: a weaker association among impaired adults supports out of reach, and a stronger one supports environmental docility. NDVI and park access are scaled per interquartile range (IQR); park presence and quality enter as four categories, with no park as the reference.

**Aim 2.** We will fit Cox proportional hazards models with age as the time scale, exposure and impairment updated at each round, robust variance clustered by person, and censoring at the last completed interview.<sup>29</sup> As in Aim 1, each model includes one nature measure, one impairment domain, and their interaction. We use interaction terms rather than a stratified Cox model, because only the former tests whether associations differ by impairment.

**Model structure.** For each outcome, we will fit one model per impairment domain and nature measure (4 × 3 = 12 models) and control the false discovery rate (FDR) across them. Because impairments often co-occur, a sensitivity model will include all four interactions together.

**Robustness checks.** **(1)** Objective sensory measures in Rounds 11–14, and self-reported hearing impairment without the hearing-aid criterion. **(2)** Participants who never moved. **(3)** Dementia models adjusted for receiving help to go out, which may substitute for nearby nature as a route out of the home. **(4)** E-values for unmeasured confounding. **(5)** Unweighted models. **(6)** Impairment fixed at each participant’s first observation, because nearby nature may itself delay impairment, which would make time-varying impairment partly a consequence of exposure.

**Power.** Interaction tests need larger samples than main effects,<sup>30</sup> and NHATS provides them: in the public files, 13,712 participants contribute 57,262 person-rounds and 3,378 deaths. Using the observed prevalence of each impairment and the within-person correlation in going out (0.47), we have 80% power to detect interactions with odds ratios of 1.15 to 1.24 per IQR of exposure for going out daily, a conservative binary version of the primary outcome, and hazard ratios of 1.16 to 1.27 for mortality (1.20 to 1.33 and 1.22 to 1.38 under a Bonferroni bound for 12 tests). The cohesion interactions in our preliminary data show that effects of this size are detectable in NHATS. Before the restricted data arrive, a plasmode simulation on the real NHATS panel will refine these estimates; domains with limited power, such as objective sensory measures in recent rounds, will be reported as exploratory.

**7. Plan for future funding**

The pilot answers the questions an R01 must settle first: which impairment domains modify nature’s associations, in which direction, and whether impairment keeps people from reaching nature. We will use the results in an NIA R01 (target submission June 2028) that collects primary data from older adults living with mobility, sensory, or mild cognitive impairment in New York City. Participants will carry global positioning system (GPS) loggers and accelerometers and complete ecological momentary assessments, short repeated surveys on a phone, over repeated two-week waves. These data will measure contact with nature directly rather than infer it from residence, and test which specific features of urban nature increase visits, activity, and perceived restoration among impaired adults. The pilot’s effect sizes will set the R01’s sample size. Including adults with mild cognitive impairment or early dementia, together with their care partners, would align the R01 with NIA’s priorities for Alzheimer’s disease and related dementias. Because the results identify modifiable features of nearby nature and individual barriers to using it, they could inform park and street design by city agencies. Future work could also explore how to make nature accessible to the senses of people with vision or hearing impairment.
