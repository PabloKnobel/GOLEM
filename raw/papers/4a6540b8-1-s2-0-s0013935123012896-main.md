#### **1. Introduction** 

A considerable proportion of the global burden of disease is attributable to environmental factors (WHO, 2016) and complex interactions between genes and the environment (Rappaport, 2011; Sankar, 2004). But what is referred to when we talk about environment? Rappaport and Smith (2010) define environment, and particularly the detrimental effects of environmental exposures, beyond pollution, climate, and noise, stating that “(…) exposures are not restricted to chemicals (toxicants) entering the body from air, water, or food, for example, but also include chemicals produced by inflammation, oxidative stress, lipid peroxidation, infections, gut flora, and other natural processes” (Rappaport and Smith, 2010, p. 460). Krieger’s eco-social theory (2011) extends this 

understanding of the scope of health-relevant environmental exposures emphasizing the relevance of the social domain: The eco-social theory postulates that humans, as social beings who live in socially organized environments, embody the ecological, material, and social worlds in which they live, including the distributions of power and wealth over time and across space (Krieger, 2011). The underlying concept of embodiment (Krieger, 2005) stresses that being engaged with the surrounding world initiates transformation of bodily characteristics through the physical incorporation of that environment with consequences for gene regulation and expression. Simultaneously, this transformation feeds back, changing this very environment. Krieger, thus, emphasizes embodiment as an active and reciprocal process that is “more than just about how social conditions ‘get under the skin’“but

rather represents “our bodily engagement (soma and psyche combined), individually and collectively, with the biophysical world and each other.” (Krieger, 2011, p. 222). Examples for embodied social environment come from research on biomarkers of chronic stress and accelerated physiologic aging such as telomere length and DNA methylation (Kawachi and Subramanian, 2018; Vineis and Kelly-Irving, 2019). For instance, in African American women, telomere attrition has recently been found to be influenced by interactions between institutional racial discrimination, educational attainment, and employment status (Thomas et al., 2021). Other examples for embodied social environment originate from research on neuroendocrine pathways in the context of the allostatic load hypothesis, linking adverse living conditions with health disparities (Johnson et al., 2017; Morello-Frosch and Shenassa, 2006; Ribeiro et al., 2018; Shankardass, 2012; Szanton et al., 2005). According to this evidence, environmental influences on health comprise biological, physical, chemical, and, particularly _social_ exposures. 

Interest in exploring the pathogenic effects of environmental exposures has a long history. In the 1970s, formal quantitative risk assessments of chemicals focused on single exposures using simplistic ‘one exposure – one outcome’ models (Sexton, 2012). Moreover, approaches of risk factor epidemiology dominated quantitative research on environmental health. Beginning in the 1980s, the need has been emphasized to develop methods to assess total exposure, taking all exposure pathways into account (Lioy, 1990; Wallace et al., 1987) and to consider cumulative and interactive health effects of simultaneous exposures from a wide range of environmental stressors (Sexton and Linder, 2011). Against the background that psychosocial stressors linked to social inequities may increase vulnerability to detrimental health effects of environmental exposures, the approach of cumulative risk assessment as a way to evaluate combined effects of multiple physical, chemical, and social stressors on health has been proposed (Payne-Sturges et al., 2018). This need to consider cumulative effects of multiple exposures has been recently culminated in the concept of the _exposome_ , conceptualized by Wild (2005, 2012) as the non-genetic counterpart to the _genome._ In his work, Wild (2012) emphasized the need to incorporate the life-time complement of the totality of all conceivable non-genetic drivers of health and disease including exposures from internal and external sources of physical, chemical, biological, and _social_ influences. Despite Wild’s holistic approach, recent guiding work in the field of exposome research continues to focus mainly on the effect of biological, physical, and chemical exposures on health and risks to overlook the relevance of the social environment in understanding disease etiology (Neufcourt et al., 2022). On the contrary, the political movement and scientific research on social health determinants and environmental justice has emphasized for a long time the interaction of environmental and social factors in producing health inequities: Environmental hazards and benefits tend to be unequally distributed, the former congregating in populations with unfavorable socioeconomic and demographic factors (Dreger et al., 2019; Evans and Kantrowitz, 2002; Fairburn et al., 2019; Schüle et al., 2019). While _health inequalities_ address general differences in health without normative judgement, unequal distributions which are assumed to be unfair, unjust, and avoidable, particularly when linked to social determinants of health, are referred to as _health inequities_ (CSDH, 2008; Kruize et al., 2014; Solar and Irwin, 2010; WHO Regional Office for Europe, 2012; WHO Regional Office for Europe, 2019a; WHO Regional Office for Europe, 2019b). The need to address the inequitable distribution of exposures to negative (environmental hazards, e.g. pollution) and positive (environmental benefits, e.g. green space) environmental health influences across population groups has been stressed in terms of distributional justice in addition to procedural justice as equal treatment and involvement of all population groups in environmental decision-making (Bolte et al., 2011; Brulle and Pellow, 2006; Gee and Payne-Sturges, 2004; Zhuang et al., 2022). Moreover, besides these exposure differentials, social differences in vulnerability to the health effects of environmental exposures (e.g. effect modification) 

have been considered as important mechanism in environmental justice research (Bolte et al., 2011; Gee and Payne-Sturges, 2004). 

Despite these converging insights that recognize the entangled dependencies between social influences and health, social influences are rarely being conceptualized in a theoretically grounded way and hardly integrated into exposome research alongside other types (e.g. physical) of exposures (Neufcourt et al., 2022; Vineis and Barouki, 2022). For instance, when searching for ‘exposome’ in the title/abstract of scientific articles up until the end of 2021, 895 publications are indexed in PubMed. This number shrinks to only five publications (Arias et al., 2022; Buekers et al., 2018; Jackson and Arah, 2020; Nwanaji-Enwerem et al., 2021; Senier et al., 2017) when the keyword ‘exposome’ is combined with the search terms ‘social*’ or ‘socio*‘. Furthermore, only two of these five publications, namely the work by Nwanaji-Enwerem et al. (2021) and Senier et al. (2017), elaborated on exposome-related concepts at all, with the idea to broaden the understanding of the exposome. Nwanaji-Enwerem et al. (2021) advertise a _compound exposome_ approach, stressing the so-called _social exposome_ as essential for a holistic view on exposure-disease pathways. In this article, the authors do list several social indicators that have been linked to health inequities (referring in particular to structural racism), however they do not elaborate on a conceptual framework of a social exposome. Thus, of these publications, only Senier et al. (2017) propose a conceptualization of social exposures in the exposome, called the _socio-exposome_ . The authors emphasize their _socio-exposome_ as a multidisciplinary conceptual framework that considers environmental exposures from individual, local, and global levels and aims to integrate sociological, public health, and environmental justice scholarship (Senier et al., 2017). The authors argue against a strongly individualized path towards a personalized medicine approach as it is typically followed in genome research, but advertise a more holistic way in order to achieve broader public health goals. One of the core principles of their framework is to link biological disease pathways and social environmental forces in order to uncover how regulatory principles (of e.g. authorities, institutions) underlie structural inequalities and environmental risk inequalities (Senier et al., 2017). By a more extended search, we identified two further publications that stress the social and psychosocial domain within the exposome concept. The _public health exposome_ from Juarez et al. (2014) highlights population-level risks across the social and policy environment, particularly stressing the socioeconomic factors with regard to exposure distributions and individual susceptibility. The authors detail their framework’s aims to integrate complex relationships between exogenous and endogenous exposures, particularly emphasizing their lifelong occurrence. The concept of the _psychoexposome_ from Colomina et al. (2018) considers psychosocial environmental exposures in the context of psychopathology, particularly addressing the imprinting effect of early life experiences on neural circuits that may adversely alter increasing susceptibility towards psychological disorders. 

These few pioneer approaches within the field of exposome research are in line with the very recent claims from exposome researchers to no longer reduce social determinants of health to confounding variables but to treat social influences as overarching determinants as they have manifold downstream effects on other exposures (Neufcourt et al., 2022 Vineis, 2022; Vineis and Barouki, 2022), as is well-known according to the profound research and conceptual framework on social determinants of health (CSDH, 2008; Friel and Marmot, 2011; WHO Regional Office for Europe, 2019a). 

Building on this work, we aim to fill the gap that exists in the social domain of exposome research. To achieve this, we extend previous approaches by directing the focus of attention on two less explored aspects: a) systematic and comprehensive characterization of the ‘totality’ of social exposures and b) transmission pathways between exposures and outcomes, particularly incorporating insights from health inequality, justice, and equity research. Combining these focus points, we propose a novel holistic conceptual framework of the _Social Exposome_ , with a twofold goal. The first goal is to bring together the breadth of social and

The second goal, in order to move beyond a mere compilation of social exposures, is to uncover potential transmission pathways that will advance our understanding of how (the entirety of) individual and contextual, structural social exposures translate into health outcomes. 

To achieve these goals, the proposed novel conceptual framework of the Social Exposome was developed in an iterative literature-based and expertise-driven research process. We, firstly, synthesized previous knowledge a) to systematize and b) to identify the social environment in order to understand structuring mechanisms that underly the interplay of the _multitude_ of exposures. This systematic consideration of social influences is an essential pre-step, as only a holistic portrayal of the human social environment – including social, psychosocial, socioeconomic, sociodemographic, local, regional, and cultural aspects – lays the fundamental step to understand how the entirety of social exposures affects health and disease. Secondly, we extended previous concepts by identifying pathways that translate social exposures into health outcomes to further understand the link between social environment and health. Particularly, insights from research on social structures and processes behind health inequalities need to be incorporated to uncover how – especially in terms of mechanisms and pathways – social inequalities in health emerge, are maintained, and systematically drive health outcomes (Bolte et al., 2021; CSDH, 2008; Friel and Marmot, 2011; Galea et al., 2011; Gkiouleka et al., 2018; Kruize et al., 2014; Ostlin et al., 2011; Oversveen et al., 2017; Solar and Irwin, 2010; WHO Regional Office for Europe, 2012). 

By means of this broad method, we aimed to comprehensively capture the available research on this topic, irrespective of the underlying disciplinary field in order to complement what is, in its conception, the holistic perspective of the exposome. The results of our research, therefore, will not only be useful to the exposome research community, but can be seen as a research interface for different disciplines ranging from social to health sciences. 

#### **2. Methods** 

An explorative approach of reviewing and synthesizing theories was applied to develop a multi-dimensional conceptual framework of the _Social Exposome_ . This approach was inspired by a three-stage procedure by Bolte et al. (2021) and adapted to the twofold goals of the current paper. The first two stages comprised reviewing literature and establishing an overview of existing conceptual approaches to describe and understand social exposures and social inequalities relevant for health. The third stage evaluated these findings and involved data synthesis and refinement of preliminary conceptual frameworks to develop a novel _Social Exposome_ conceptual framework. The entire development process lasted from 2020 to 2022. 

This conceptual paper emerged from the collaborative research project Equal-Life (van Kamp et al., 2022) and presents a newly developed conceptual framework of the Social Exposome. The exposome perspective on children’s and adolescents’ mental health and cognitive development will be specifically covered by another conceptual paper of the Equal-Life consortium (Persson Waye et al., in preparation for this special issue). Equal-Life comprises expertise from 21 European research institutions from 11 European countries and aims to gain insight on the profound impact of physical and social environment on health, looking in particular at children’s and young people’s mental health and cognitive development (van Kamp et al., 2022). 

_2.1. Stage One: Literature search on theory-based concepts of the social domain_ 

In the first stage, we assessed the current state of literature regarding theory-based concepts of social influences relevant for health. There were three underlying methodological considerations that structured 

this work. Firstly, based on our expert knowledge in the fields of social epidemiology, social determinants of health, and environmental justice, we identified a number of influential texts that have been widely acknowledged as seminal and groundbreaking in the scientific community of social and health disciplines (e.g. Bolte et al., 2010; Brulle and Pellow, 2006; CSDH, 2008; Evans and Kantrowitz, 2002; Gee and Payne-Sturges, 2004; Gkiouleka et al., 2018; Hankivsky et al., 2017 Krieger, 2011; Marmot et al., 2012; Morello-Frosch and Shenassa, 2006 Whitehead et al., 2016; WHO Regional Office for Europe, 2019a). Secondly, in the ensuing ‘snowballing process’ (Bolte et al., 2021) or ‘pearl-growing approach’ (Whitehead et al., 2016), the literature lists of these selected publications were hand searched to identify additional relevant publications. Thirdly, this identification of relevant publications iteratively continued until theoretical saturation was reached, i.e. further reading did not gain additional theoretical insights. This final selection of texts was used as a theoretical foundation in order to set the scope for a broad spectrum of health-related social exposures and to compile a set of systematization categories needed to conceptually structure social exposures. Four categories were identified: a) type of exposures, b) setting of exposures, c) timing of exposures, d) exposure patterning. These derived categories were used to systematize social exposures in the subsequent **Stage Two** , where we conducted a scoping review to capture the broadness of the social domain. 

The first two categories address the quality and extent of exposures, referring a) _to what_ and b) _in which settings_ individuals can be exposed. The timing category c) relates to _when_ and _how often_ exposures occur, particularly considering the life-course approach. The life-course perspective stresses the cumulative effect of exposures with regard to longitudinal health effects as well as sensitive or critical periods of disease development (Cable, 2014; Kuh et al., 2003; Lynch and Smith, 2005). Already when first introducing the exposome concept, Wild (2005) emphasized the need to consider the multitude of non-genetic exposures of the entire life-course from conception onwards in order to understand the progression of health and disease. Contrary to the first three individual-level categories, the category d) exposure patterning represents the population-level of social exposures, i.e. the embeddedness of individual experiences into the broader (structural) context. This contextual category refers to structural mechanisms that underly inequities in developmental and health pathways. Within this patterning contextual category, we particularly included the societal structure _gender_ from an _intersectionality_ perspective (Hankivsky et al., 2017; Merz et al., 2021) as an important factor contributing to health inequities (Krieger, 2003). 

_2.2._ **_Stage Two:_** _Systematic overview of social and psychosocial exposures_ 

To systematically identify concepts and frameworks that assess the social and psychosocial environment and to inform the development of our conceptual framework, a scoping review was conducted in accordance with PRISMA ScR guidelines (Tricco et al., 2018). The search syntax included a wide range of keywords and was designed for two sub-searches: a) Social environment and b) Psychosocial environment. This subdivision was introduced to ensure that psychosocial influences, as an integral feature of the social environment, will not be missed due to potential differences in the use of terminology across scientific disciplines. For the subsequent screening, records of both sub-searches were merged. The search syntax required key words to occur in the title and/or abstract. Records were limited to review articles published in peer reviewed journals or as book chapters between January 2000 and August 2020. Only research contributions limited to humans and published in English were considered. The search covered three databases that differed in their research disciplines and emphasis: PubMed (health sciences), Scopus (bio, social, natural, health sciences) and PsycInfo (psychology). #### _3.1. Multidimensionality_ 

The Social Exposome conceptual framework is based on the key assumption that the entirety of a person’s social environment systematically impacts upon development and health. This social environment ranges from the immediate social environment (individual-level), up to the broadest societal context (population-level) in which the individuallevel social experiences are embedded. These multiple dimensions, visually represented as bedded circles and intersecting triangles around the centrally placed individual (Fig. 1), symbolize the constant presence of a huge variety of social exposures that, furthermore, continuously evolve over time. The core principle of multidimensionality in this conceptual framework stresses the idea that these exposures all together determine the quality of the social environment and that the social environment cannot be adequately described by considering only a single attribute from the spectrum of social exposures. For a more detailed explanation see legends to Figs. 1 and 2, Table 2 and Supplement 1.

The explicit characterization of social interactions is essential to understand the effect of the social environment, not only because social interactions link the individual with the outer world but also because of the significance of social relationships for health and development. The body of evidence examining the association between social interactions and health outcomes converges on the assumption that social relationships have a considerable impact on well-being (e.g. Earls and Carlson, 2001), mental health (e.g. Seeman and Crimmins, 2001), physical health (e.g. Umberson and Montez, 2010), and longevity and mortality (e.g. Holt-Lunstad, 2018; Seeman and Crimmins, 2001) to name only a few outcomes. The recent _Social Baseline Theory_ from the field of neuroscience suggests that social (supportive relationships) and metabolic (oxygen, glucose) resources are treated almost interchangeably by the human brain, particularly in socially challenging situations (Coan and Sbarra, 2015). The authors claim that the presence of social relationships (measured in terms of interdependence, shared goals, joint attention) is expected in the brain’s default state, while their absence is perceived as lacking available resources and results in physiological and cognitive effort and negative consequences for health and well-being (Coan and Sbarra, 2015).

#### _3.1.2. Impact of social inequalities and structural injustices on social environment dimensions_ 

The introduced multiple dimensions together and in interaction with each other make up the social environment of individuals and populations. However, it is too simplistic to assume, that social environments should result in a great diversity due to a mere infinite variety of individualized experiences. Instead, social environments follow rather conceivable trajectories, because opportunities and their accessibility are typically clustered with regard to population groups rather than individuals. These structural phenomena, in the Social Exposome conceptual framework, are captured as **Social inequalities and Structural injustices** . Social inequalities refer to population-level differences in social conditions; these inequalities are not mere consequences of differing socioeconomic and sociodemographic aspects, but are systematically maintained and aggravated by economic, political, and sociocultural forces that define distribution patterns of risks (distributive justice) and access to resources and opportunities for taking action and participation in processes of decision-making (procedural justice). In this way, they ultimately determine structural inequities and injustices in circumstances in which people grow, live, work, and age (CSDH, 2008). For example, decisions regarding welfare support, economic policy, public spending, the provision of education and health care services, and housing policy and provision often disadvantage those on lower incomes by disincentives on entering (or increasing) employment for those earning less (Marmot et al., 2010). Furthermore, social norms and cultural values not only inform societal regulations in terms of the systems, institutions, and priorities of the political economy, they can also influence the construction, definition, and perception of particular groups in a society that may be distinguished based on certain characteristics and disadvantaged as a result of structural injustices. This dynamic can create potential for discrimination or stigmatization (Gkiouleka et al., 2018; Krieger, 2020; Schulz and Northridge, 2004). For example, children and parents who do not correspond to a particular social norm around family structure (e.g. nuclear family norm) or cultural integration (e.g. language barrier) may experience discrimination that may also contribute to social exclusion. At the cultural level, several examples of values exist in literature in terms of how discrimination in its varied forms is perceived, tolerated, and perpetuated. For instance, ‘uncertainty avoidance’ is one of the cultural principles Hofstede (2001) 

introduced to understand cross-cultural differences. Avoidance of, or an aversion to, uncertainty can relate to obedience to rules, regulations, and also to conventional ways of thinking, typically taking skeptical positions towards unknown and unusual circumstances or attitudes. ‘Individualism and collectivism’ can broadly be understood as cultural preferences for social frameworks within which willingness towards taking care of others outside of yourself and your immediate family may be valued differently (Hofstede, 2001). 

Crucially, social inequities can accumulate with regard to severity of disadvantage through intersecting risks and unequally distributed power relations, a concept called **Intersectionality** . Hankivsky et al. (2017) define intersectionality “as a framework which focuses on the ways in which multiple axes of social inequality intersect and co-construct one another at the macro and micro levels to produce a broad range of unequal outcomes, in both individual and population health. These interactions occur within a context of connected systems and structures of power (e.g. laws, policies, state governments and other political and economic unions, religious institutions, media). Through such processes, interdependent forms of privilege and oppression are shaped by colonialism, imperialism, racism, homophobia, ableism, and patriarchy.” (Hankivsky et al., 2017, p. 3). The intersectionality perspective has been particularly linked to recognizing gender as one of the most important social determinants of health (e.g. Greaves and Ritz, 2022; Krieger, 2005; Merz et al., 2021). The multidimensional construct gender is understood to be culturally constructed and context specific. Four dimensions of gender can be distinguished: Gender identity, gender roles, gender relations (how individuals interact with and are treated by others), and institutionalized gender (the distribution of power between the genders) (Greaves and Ritz, 2022; Johnson et al., 2009). These gender dimensions together with their potential interactions have to be considered in analyses of health effects (Krieger, 2003). As conceptualized by the embodiment theory, the entanglement of sex and gender is indicated by the use of the term “sex/gender” (Springer et al., 2012). However, despite the progressing debate of the need to integrate sex/gender to ensure validity of health research and substantial insights from the intersectionality approach for health equity research (Bolte et al., 2021; Merz et al., 2021), sex/gender is often either regarded as a fixed innate individual characteristic in conceptual models of social determinants of health (Pearce et al., 2019; WHO Regional Office for Europe, 2006) or even neglected in health sciences and in the exposome research field (e.g. Colomina et al., 2018; Juarez et al., 2014; Nwanaji-Enwerem et al., 2021; Senier et al., 2017; Turner et al., 2017; Vermeulen et al., 2020; Wild, 2005; Wild, 2012). 

In sum, the multidimensionality of the Social Exposome conceptual framework focuses on the entirety of a person’s social environment for their development and health. The simultaneous consideration of multiple embedded dimensions of exposures highlights how the interplay of social influences, as stressed by the intersectionality perspective, underpins privilege and discrimination in societal systems. In this interplay, the relevance of explicit and non-explicit rules and laws, norms, and values is emphasized as central structural drivers of structural inequities and injustices. 

#### _3.2. Reciprocity_ 

The core principle of reciprocity addresses the interrelations between multiple co-existing dimensions at individual and population levels. Our understanding of reciprocity is in line with the systems approach. positing that a (social) system is characterized by the interdependence of its parts, while also having the properties of the system defined by these parts and vice versa (Reis et al., 2000). Accordingly, the embeddedness of multiple dimensions of exposures in the Social Exposome are understood as being inherently interlinked, meaning that they determine the quality of the social environment not only together but also interactively. For example, social norms and cultural values affect systems, institutions, and priorities of the political economy. These, for their part,

Moreover, in the Social Exposome conceptual framework, the reciprocity principle covers not only reciprocity between exposures, but also the reciprocal interlinkages between exposures and their effect on health outcomes. To exemplify such linkages and to shed light on how transmission between exposures and health outcomes may work, in the Social Exposome conceptual framework a person’s health can be regarded as the visible ‘outside’ – figuratively speaking – of what actually shapes and is shaped in interaction with the social environment – figuratively speaking – the ‘inside’ of a person. A selection of such shaping/shaped internal attributes is represented within the centrally placed inner figure in the visual representation of the Social Exposome framework (Fig. 2). For example, ‘personality’ is understood as a highly individual characteristic set of cognitive, emotional, and behavioral patterns (Briley and Tucker-Drob, 2014). Theories on development of personality stress its stability over time that endure lifespan trends, while simultaneously acknowledging the guiding influence of exogenous forces, most notably the social environment (for an overview, see Briley and Tucker-Drob, 2014). Personality can also be considered as an antecedent that frames beliefs and opinions (Brown and Cinamon, 2016). In this way, personality affects health-relevant characteristics, including, for instance, Antonovsky’s (1993) _sense of coherence_ (a global orientation that the challenges with which an individual is confronted in the course of life are perceived as comprehensible, manageable, and meaningful), optimism/pessimism, intelligence, self-efficacy, and interpersonal skills (e. g. communication, openness, kindness) (e.g. Holling and Schlack, 2008¨ ; Taylor and Seeman, 1999). Thus, personality does not only develop (i.e. is shaped) in interaction with the social environment, but also affects (i. e. shapes) how social environment evolves, thereby affecting health outcomes. Similarly, increased **stress sensitivity** refers to an enhanced reactivity of physiological stress circuits. The stress response is essential for adaptation, maintenance of homeostasis, and survival. Stress exposure has been shown to induce a state of neuroendocrinological hypervigilance, characterized by a highly sensitive, yet unspecific, neural processing (e.g. Henckens et al., 2016). Recurrent adverse stress events and chronic stress, particularly in infancy and childhood, permanently affect the neuroendocrinological stress responsivity and lower the threshold for tolerating future stressful events, which has been strongly linked to a variety of psychopathology (e.g. Bale, 2006; Wade et al., 2019). **Identity** , the sense of belonging and who someone is, in distinction as well as in relation to others, also evolves in social interactions that are embedded into the broader context. This is also true for **knowledge** as well as for **skills and abilities** . The later include cognitive, social, and emotional competencies to function in a given environment (Galderisi et al., 2015). All these examples of inside 

attributes not only develop in interaction with others but, as social relationships themselves, are demographically patterned and socially constructed (Umberson and Montez, 2010). 

The idea of health as a reciprocal process of ‘shaping and being shaped’ can be found in several conceptual models. For example, in 2011, Huber and colleagues proposed a dynamic view of health based on the “ability to adapt and to self-manage” (Huber et al., 2011). In this regard, health is a steadily evolving process of adjustment towards the requirements of an ever-changing environment. The authors introduced the concept of _social health_ to complement physical and mental health concepts. Social health is characterized “as a dynamic balance between opportunities and limitations, shifting through life and affected by external conditions such as social and environmental challenges” (Huber et al., 2011). Similarly, within the Social Exposome conceptual framework, social exposures can be both adverse as well as beneficial. The evolving and dynamic nature of a person’s social environment is visually represented in our conceptual framework by interlocked wheels _(_ Figs. 1 and 2 _)_ . The interlocked wheels symbolize critical life events along with substantial changes in life circumstances (e.g. developmental challenges over the life course such as starting school and puberty, loss or death of a close person, maltreatment) as well as everyday challenges and demands (e.g. family functioning, poverty). Within the Social Exposome conceptual framework the interlocked wheels, thus, can be understood as both positive (adaptive) and negative (maladaptive) living conditions that are faced in interaction with other people of the surrounding environment but also in interaction with prevailing opportunities (e.g. support, structural accessibility) and that reciprocally affect health by either weakening or strengthening current conditions. 

A major focus within the Social Exposome conceptual framework is to shed light on how exposures of the social and psychosocial environment are actually translated into health outcomes in this reciprocal process. We propose three such transmission pathways: **Embodiment** , **Resilience and Susceptibility or Vulnerability,** and **Empowerment.** 

Krieger (2005) defines **Embodiment** as “a concept referring to how we literally incorporate, biologically, the material and social world in which we live, from in utero to death; a corollary is that no aspect of our biology can be understood in the absence of knowledge of history and individual and societal ways of living.” (Krieger, 2005, p. 353). Thus, being continuously engaged with the surrounding social world can initiate transformation processes of physical characteristics relevant for health. For example, a society’s gender norms, embodied in structures and institutions, can enforce the gender binary (female/male) and therefore privilege particular groups whose gender expression is heterosexual, cisgender, and gender-conforming (Krieger, 2020). Furthermore, embodiment of social factors culminates in embodiment of social inequality, as discussed by Gravlee (2009) with regard to health inequalities between racially defined groups. 

**Resilience** , here mainly referred to as an internal trait, is understood as a dynamic process of positive adaptation and refers to recovery from adverse experiences. It enables an individual to develop into a healthy member of a community despite (recurrent) negative experiences, trauma and crisis (Werner, 1989). More recent notions of resilience emphasize the developing process of psychological maturing in the sense of ‘posttraumatic growth’ as a result of undergoing and overcoming emotional and/or physical burden (Wessa, 2019). Resilience, thus, not only refers to the process of adaptation, but is also an adaptive resource that evolves in interaction with challenges of life. In line with other adaptive resources, resilience is dynamic, meaning that its protective function can be depleted and requires periodic restoration. Restoration refers to processes to renew, recover, or re-establish adaptive capacities that deplete in ongoing daily efforts (Hartig, 2004). Particularly challenging phases with few restorative moments are characterized by low resilience and go hand in hand with increased susceptibility and vulnerability. **Susceptibility** refers to an individual’s internal receptiveness to adverse influences, e.g. biological predispositions, thus modifying health effects of exposures (Morello-Frosch et al., 2011). **Vulnerability** is a potential amplifier of negative health consequences through environmental hazards and also modifies the health effects of external factors (Bolte et al., 2018). The vulnerability concept has been applied in multiple disciplines and is highly field-dependent in its definition, ranging from ecological to regional through to social vulnerability. However, the common ground of these concepts of vulnerability is the focus on the effects of external factors on individuals, populations, communities, and systems (Bolte et al., 2018). Within environmental justice research, community-level vulnerability (e.g. community stressors, neighborhood resources, social infrastructure) and individual-level vulnerability (e.g. coping capacities, preexisting medical conditions) have been distinguished (Bolte et al., 2018; Gee and Payne-Sturges, 2004). Crucially, the strength of the effect of a specific external factor depends on individual characteristics of those who are exposed to this external factor (e.g. pre-existing diseases, psychosocial stress, malnutrition, cumulative burdens), reflecting vulnerability’s essential property of effect-modification (Bolte et al., 2018; WHO Regional Office for Europe, 2019b). A prominent example comes from the field of air pollution epidemiology, where effect modification by socioeconomic status is considered as an area of growing importance (Hajat et al., 2021). An early review by Laurent et al. (2007) indicated that especially studies with individual-level socioeconomic data showed that effects of air pollution on mortality were greater among poorer people. Differential vulnerability has been conceptualized as one of the main causal pathways of environmental health inequity (Bolte et al., 2010). 

**Empowerment** , at the individual level, refers to the individual perception and experience of the own self as encouraged, skilled, and competent to approach and take charge of own concerns, both in private and in public. First attempts of a scientific definition emphasize the process of change (individual, interpersonal, or interactional), that enables individuals to master their environment and achieve selfdetermination (Simmons and Parsons, 1983), both, at individual and community levels (Rissel, 1994). In environmental justice research, personal empowerment has been defined as a key to enable participation of citizens in environmental decision-making, thus contributing to 

procedural justice (Schlosberg, 2007). This transmission pathway is particularly relevant with regards to **Social interactions** in the conceptual framework of the Social Exposome: Agency (e.g. in a family context), community participation (e.g. in a neighborhood action group), and civic engagement (e.g. in a grassroots initiative on environmental justice) reflect different domains at which empowerment can be realized in interpersonal relationships. Empowerment, however, is not only a personal skill but is tightly interlinked with access and available opportunities for taking action. Procedural (in)justice refers to existing differences between population groups with regard to opportunities to influence decisions affecting their local environment (WHO Regional Office for Europe, 2012; WHO Regional Office for Europe, 2019a). Thus, restricted access and social exclusion not only impair agency, participation, and engagement (Galderisi et al., 2015), but they are also the root cause for social and health inequalities that may manifest in structural injustices, by either enabling or prohibiting capacity for action. Therefore, the WHO considers empowerment as one of the main strategies to fight inequities (Evans and Van Lerberghe, 2008). 

In this way, the reciprocity of the conceptual framework highlights the interrelatedness of the multiple dimensions of the Social Exposome that reciprocally feed forward and feed back, interactively shaping the quality of the social environment, and in this way translating exposures into different (though interrelated) health outcomes. 

#### _3.3. Timing and continuity_ 

Within the Social Exposome conceptual framework, the core principles of multidimensionality and reciprocity are additionally backed by a principle concerning the temporal domain: Timing and continuity. The Social Exposome is intended to be dynamic and changeable in how it incorporates multiple dimensions and their variability throughout an individual’s lifetime such that it can be used to continuously structure environmental exposures along with the life course. The life course, here, refers to “the social pathways defined by events and transitions experienced by individuals and the sequences of roles and experiences followed by individuals over particular phases of their lives” (Alwin and Wray, 2005, p. 9). The life course approach to health takes account of strong evidence that links the origins of numerous adult mental and physical health conditions to early life by recognizing the importance of early life exposures as essential risk and protective processes for later health outcomes and stressing the entire life-course as being health-relevant (e.g. Cable, 2014; Kuh et al., 2003; Lynch and Smith, 2005; Niedzwiedz et al., 2012; Richter et al., 2018). In order to understand the longitudinal effects of exposures at various developmental stages, the life-course approach highlights two central temporal aspects: critical windows for risk exposure (timing) and accumulation of risk over time (continuity) (Cable, 2014; Kuh et al., 2003; Lynch and Smith, 2005). 

Barker’s (1998) _biological programming hypothesis_ (also fetal origins hypothesis) can be regarded as one of the conceptual cornerstones of the life-course approach. According to this hypothesis, in utero development can be (irreversibly) affected if mother or embryo/fetus are exposed to risk, e.g. pollution, malnutrition, social neglect, or mother’s psychological stress during pregnancy (for an overview, see e.g. Payne-Sturges et al., 2021), during windows of critical timing. Critical timing refers to periods during which organ structures and associated functions undergo biological programming, which presets physiological and metabolic responses that carry into adulthood (Barker, 1998). Similarly, both adverse and beneficial postnatal (early) life experiences at critical time points over the course of development affect a range of physiological processes. These include the organism’s regulatory systems (e.g. stress, inflammatory, metabolic), neural mechanisms underlying such systems, and the related expression of genes and neural responses, eventually affecting adult mental and physical health even decades later (e.g. Shonkoff et al., 2009; Taylor, 2010). Crucially, the influential (early) life experiences affect the entire life-course from conception onwards and

In addition to critical timing of risk exposure, the life-course approach also emphasizes the continuity of time, which results in accumulation of risk (e.g. Kuh et al., 2003; Lupien et al., 2009; Richter et al., 2018). The focus here is on the dynamic, increasing adversity of consecutive and/or simultaneously occurring multiple risk exposures. Cumulative risk effects typically result in socially patterned health pathways. For example, individual life trajectories are often characterized by repeatedly facing either predominantly positive or negative situations, in which “one risk factor may lead to a chain reaction of additional risk (e.g. financial insecurity can cause strain on relationships, leading to separation and in turn greater financial strain)” (Pearce et al., 2019, p. 4). Hence, the life-course approach is particularly relevant with regard to addressing social inequalities and structural injustices and how these are realized at different life stages, in particular how they are transmitted from stage to stage within the own life course (intra-generational social inequalities) and even between generations (inter-generational social inequalities) (Lofters and O’Campo, 2012). 

The principle of timing and continuity of the Social Exposome conceptual framework highlights how tracing the life-long social environment (both the timing and duration of exposures as well as their cumulative nature) may help to systematically approach evolving health pathways. Considering social inequalities and structural injustices may play a particular role in identifying meaningful intersections or timepoints along the life course where health pathways diverge, revealing options for intervention. 

#### **4. Discussion** 

Building on research from various disciplines including public health, social epidemiology, environmental health research, environmental and developmental psychology, and environmental medicine, and particularly considering previously published concepts on social exposures, social determinants of health, health equity, and intersectionality perspective, we propose a novel conceptual framework of the Social Exposome. This conceptual framework was developed in an iterative literature-based and expertise-driven research process to add to the understanding of the social environment, its impact on health, and relevant transmitting pathways within exposome research. 

The Social Exposome concept provides a holistic framework on how the social environment – including social, psychosocial, socioeconomic, sociodemographic, local, regional, and cultural aspects, and considering individual as well as contextual exposures – in its totality systematically impacts upon health, in contrast to selectively addressing individual social influences. Up to now, an all-encompassing theory-based conceptualization that comprehensively captures and integrates the social domain within exposome research has been lacking. The conceptual framework of the Social Exposome was developed to fill this gap. In the process of development of the Social Exposome conceptual framework our focus was on understanding the underlying mechanisms that underpin the transformation of a mere multitude of exposures towards a holistic exposome paradigm – the Social Exposome. Three structuring core principles are emphasized: Multidimensionality, Reciprocity, and Timing and continuity. The principle of multidimensionality informs the key idea of the exposome approach to capture the entirety, i.e. all conceivable dimensions of the social environment, ranging from the most immediate exposures (social interactions) to the broadest societal context. The claim for entirety is distinctly opposed from an approach of risk factor epidemiology that often analyzes the independent effect of single variables (e.g. socioeconomic characteristics), conceptualized as individual (static) characteristics in relation to health outcomes. The principle of reciprocity stresses the interrelatedness between different environmental exposures and between environment and outcomes. Only by recognizing their reciprocal nature we can 

shed light on how exposures determine positive and negative health effects. To advance our comprehension on how (social) exposures are connected to health effects, we propose three transmission pathways: Embodiment, Resilience and Susceptibility or Vulnerability, and Empowerment. These pathways give insight into mechanisms that underlie how aspects of the social environment are translated into health outcomes. By clearly indicating the relationships between the immediate surrounding at teh individual level and the broader societal context, our conceptual framework offers the possibility to think about potential entry points for intervention and disruption of vicious circuits and chains of effects at intermediate and structural levels (and not mainly or only at the individual level). Finally, the principle of timing and continuity takes account of the steadily evolving social environment. To understand the effects of social exposures, it is relevant to consider their time point of occurrence (critical time windows) as well as their accumulation over time. For this reason, the principle of timing and continuity is essentially relevant in understanding causes and consequences of social inequalities and structural injustices. 

#### _4.1. Challenges in the field of exposome research_ 

Since the conceptualization of the _exposome_ by Wild (2005), the body of literature on exposome research has been tremendously growing. Yet, as Haddad et al. (2019) underscored in their scoping review on the characteristics of human exposome studies: “First, it is noteworthy that the number of exposome-related reviews, perspectives, and commentaries (n = 215) was exceptionally high compared with the number of publications presenting original exposome research, highlighting the ongoing theoretical interest and debate about the exposome’s utility and applications.” (Haddad et al., 2019). This crucial observation highlights the unquestioned relevance of the exposome concept. Simultaneously, and possibly even more important, this citation puts in a nutshell challenges regarding the practicability of the exposome in its conception as the _totality_ of environmental exposures. It is out of doubt that major methodological, computational, and technological advances move forward scientific opportunities concerning data assessment, data sharing, data analyses, and interpretation. Examples of these advances that can be used in exposome studies are the growing pool of methodologies and technologies including geolocation technologies, portable and personal sensing (e.g. Turner et al., 2017), public participatory methods (e.g. Juarez et al., 2014), and network science approaches that have been proven in the fields of omics research (e.g. Vermeulen et al., 2020). However, the challenging practicability of the exposome concept remains a major obstacle in the progressing exposome research. For example, several recent publications that claim to offer application-related insights about the nature of the exposome, in fact, are often rarely based on conceptually rigorous theoretical work but rather focus on nuanced specifications about a particular disease’s exposome, such as the exposome in inflammatory bowel disease (Ananthakrishnan, 2014), liver disease (Cheung et al., 2020), asthma (Guillien et al., 2021), or skin aging (Krutmann et al., 2017). With regard to the social domain, such a narrowing on single factors is even more profound. Typically, any mention of social determinants in the context of these specialized exposomes is either entirely missing (Cheung et al., 2020), limited to only few specific social aspects (e.g. psychosocial stress, as in Ananthakrishnan, 2014; Krutmann et al., 2017), or to not further specified terms such as “social factors” (Guillien et al., 2021). In more generic concepts of the exposome (e.g. Vermeulen et al., 2020 Vineis et al., 2020; Vrijheid, 2014; Zhang et al., 2021) or recent comprehensive data analysis endeavours (e.g. Maitre et al., 2022a, b Ohanyan et al., 2022; Pearson et al., 2022; Sum et al., 2022), social aspects at the individual or contextual levels were considered, but are typically not embedded in a comprehensive theoretical framework of multiple, interacting social dimensions of the exposome. Thus, recent reviews on the integration of social exposures in exposome research demonstrated again the lack of a comprehensive conceptualization of

#### _4.2. The Social Exposome in the field of exposome research_ 

The interconnection between exposures in the physical environment and the social environment and their collective impact on health (and specifically, health inequalities) is the core of research on environmental health inequalities and environmental justice. It has recently also received increasing attention in exposome research (e.g. Deguen et al., 2022). However, the majority of exposome research until today largely neglects the complexity of individual- and contextual-level social exposures. Against this background, we developed the Social Exposome conceptual framework to complement the social environment in conjunction with the physical environment within the exposome research. The interrelatedness of social and physical exposures is mirrored in socially driven clustering of environmental risks that is referred to as _exposure differentials_ (Bolte et al., 2010; Evans and Kantrowitz, 2002; Friel and Marmot, 2011; WHO Regional Office for Europe, 2012; Zhuang et al., 2022). Similarly, physical environmental exposures for their part also foster a clustering of social attributes that enhance potential inequalities, for example by limiting access to and usage of social resources (e.g. public healthcare services, educational institutions). In this respect, a recent publication claims the need for methods that can capture how accessible a given destination is to individuals not just considering physical distance alone, but also taking into account the individual’s age (and, thus, their differing mobility behavior, mobility options), a concept the authors introduce as co-accessibility (Milias and Psyllidis, 2022). Particularly linking environmental justice literature and exposome research, we see the investigation of these and other mechanisms that underly health and health inequalities as being central to interrupt otherwise persistent social inequalities and structural injustices. 

_Differential vulnerability_ , to distinguish from _exposure differentials_ , refers to modifications of health effects of a given environmental exposure through social exposures (that are differently distributed; effect modification) (Bolte et al., 2011; Bolte et al., 2010; Gee and Payne-Sturges, 2004; Morello-Frosch and Shenassa, 2006; WHO Regional Office for Europe, 2012). In line with our presentation of **Vulnerability** as one of three transmission pathways (Embodiment, Resilience and Susceptibility or Vulnerability, and Empowerment) through which social determinants in our conceptual framework can 

translate into health outcomes, Deguen et al. (2022) acknowledged in their recent review vulnerability as a mechanism that can be used to explain differential impact of exposures to environmental factors across the life-course. However, Deguen et al. (2022) did not extend the idea of vulnerability to being a mechanism that is in reciprocal interaction with a person’s social environment as a whole. Rather, vulnerability to health effects of environmental factors is discussed only in relation to socioeconomic status. The importance of a variety and combinations of social factors for health effects of environmental exposures needs to be investigated in further research so that policy decisions and interventions are better equipped to address differential vulnerability. The proposed conceptual framework of the Social Exposome can contribute to this by facilitating compelling exposome research to consider this problem not only in relation to socioeconomic factors, but also to other social and psychosocial aspects. Tracing a person’s social environment and integrating this alongside the physical environment within the exposome may help to systematically approach evolving health pathways and identify meaningful intersections where health pathways diverge in response to social inequalities and structural injustices. Health equity and environmental justice research, respectively, and exposome research should no longer operate in separate silos. We developed this conceptual framework with a vision that it could be used as the basis for a more comprehensive and balanced operationalization of social exposures in exposome research. Forthcoming research activities in the Equal-Life project (van Kamp et al., 2022) provide a unique opportunity to test the implementation of the multidimensional Social Exposome concept in the entanglement with exposures from the built and physical environment. These comprehensive approaches will be used to show how data from existing school and cohort studies, comprised in Equal-Life, can be used to operationalize aspects of the Social Exposome framework and provide new insights about the relevance of this breadth of exposures from the social environment in relation to mental health and cognitive development of children and young people. These results will be used to inform recommendations for interventions that the stakeholders involved in collaborative processes within the Equal-Life project can use to improve living environments of children and young people in urban settings. In this way, we see the conceptual framework of the Social Exposome detailed in this paper, and its first applications in Equal-Life, as a first implementation for environmental research projects to show how we can move towards a more comprehensive and theory-based integration of social factors and of the concept of social determinants of health in exposome-based environmental research projects.

In this way, our conceptual model – which embeds **Social inequalities and Structural injustices** as part of an individual’s broader context alongside other exposures from within a person’s social environment – can be seen as a conceptual building block for further work in this field. 

To demonstrate the practicability of the concept in exposome research and to validate the Social Exposome framework with regard to the proposed transmission mechanisms and health pathways was beyond the scope of this paper. The first practical applications of the conceptual framework or parts of it in statistical analyses using data from environmental epidemiological cohort studies and school studies of children and young people in Europe are planned in the forthcoming research of our Equal-Life project (van Kamp et al., 2022). These empirical research applications of the framework will contribute to its improvement through validation and refinement. 

#### **5. Conclusion** 

We propose a novel conceptual framework of the Social Exposome. Up to now, a thorough theory-based conceptualization that comprehensively captures and integrates the manifold social domain within the exposome research has been lacking. The conceptual framework of the Social Exposome was developed to fill this gap. Based on the synthesis of reviewed literature and relevant social and psychosocial theories that informed our work, we incorporated the entirety of social exposures and focused on the underlying mechanisms from health equity, environmental justice, intersectionality, and sex/gender perspectives. We outlined social exposure dimensions and three core principles that underpin the proposed conceptual framework: Multidimensionality, Reciprocity, and Timing and continuity. In this framework, transmission from social exposures to health development across the life span operates via Embodiment, Resilience and Susceptibility or Vulnerability, and Empowerment. Our conceptual framework may serve as a strategic map for both research and intervention planning aiming to decode the impact of the complex social environment and to alter transmission pathways to minimize health risks and health inequalities and to foster equity in health.
