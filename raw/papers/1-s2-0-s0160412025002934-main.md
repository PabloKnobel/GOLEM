## **1. Introduction** 

The environment and human health nexus have been extensively studied, with substantial evidence demonstrating, for instance, adverse effects of air pollution for various health outcomes (Brunekreef and Holgate, 2002; WHO, 2016a). Similarly, adverse health effects have been attributed to noise (Welch et al., 2023; Sørensen et al., 2024), high and low ambient temperature (Bunker et al., 2016), lack of residential green or limited access to green space (WHO, 2016b; Barboza et al., 2021), and light-at-night (LAN) (Bozejko et al., 2023˙ ). However, these environmental stressors do not occur independently, but rather are concurrent exposures individuals encounter throughout the day, year and their life course. Consequently, complex correlations between different environmental exposures are often observed (Ohanyan et al., 2022; Beulens et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2023). 

Exposome, defined as the totality of environment to which an individual is exposed from conception onwards (Wild, 2005), allows the estimation of the joint health effect of environmental factors (ecosystems, lifestyle, social and physical–chemical) that is of interest. The external exposome can be defined as the exposures assessed before entering the body (Turner et al., 2017). To date, an increasing number of exposome studies have been undertaken linking a broad range of external exposome factors with health (Ohanyan et al., 2022; Maitre 

et al., 2018; Agier et al., 2019; de Bont et al., 2023; Nobile et al., 2024). 

To analyze joint effects of the external exposome, epidemiological studies in multiple countries are especially useful. The Exposomics and Helix projects, for example, have pioneered exposome research in Europe (Vineis et al., 2017; Vrijheid et al., 2014). These studies encountered several challenges in elucidating health effects when considering the external exposome. One major challenge is the availability of exposure data and maps with sufficient spatial and temporal coverage across Europe, as illustrated by a recent study reporting on city-level external exposome data for 2017–2020 for 10 European cities (Tagliaferro et al., 2024). While local or national environmental models exist, integrating these into pan-European multi-cohort studies is complex because the epidemiological findings can depend on the underlying exposure model, exposure distributions and their interrelationships. A second challenge is the correlation between different exposure factors. Ignoring these correlations, or relying on single-exposure studies, can lead to imprecise conclusions about health effects. Multicollinearity, if ignored, can lead to unstable effect estimates, and make it difficult to separate the effects of individual exposures in multi-exposure models (Agier et al., 2016; Weisskopf et al., 2018). A third challenge is temporal trends in external exposures, potentially leading to temporal misalignment if exposure maps do not match the follow-up period of epidemiological studies, e.g. because often only recent exposure models are available (Strak et al., 2021). For air pollutants, several studies have documented stability of spatial exposure contrasts across time (i.e. years), but mostly for specific countries and pollutants (Eeftens et al., 2011; Gulliver et al., 2016; de Hoogh et al., 2018). There is some evidence in the Netherlands to suggest that walkability is relatively stable over time (Timmermans et al., 2021) while the food environment is subject to change (Pinho et al., 2020). Less is known about stability of other environmental stressors and their interrelations.

The project includes health data from a variety of administrative, children and adult cohorts encompassing over 25 million individuals across Europe. As a foundational part of the project, we developed a comprehensive geographical database of external exposome factors for Europe, spanning 2000–2020, to capture spatial and temporal variations. We developed new models for several exposures, including air pollution, temperature and noise (Bussalleu et al., 2024; Shen et al., 2022; Shen et al., 2024; Shen et al., 2024). A critical requirement was that datasets needed to be harmonised, to facilitate pan-European comparisons and health analyses and to meet the epidemiological needs in terms of temporal (as far back as the year 2000) and spatial resolution. 

In this exposure paper we: 1) describe the external exposome factors created in the EXPANSE project, including newly developed models for exposures where existing models or measurements were inadequate, 2) investigate the correlations between the external exposome factors and 3) assess spatial and temporal stability of these factors. Analyses were conducted at two geographic scales: Europe-wide and country level. In this paper we therefore detail different external exposome factors that can be used in epidemiological studies. 

## **2. Material and methods** 

We selected the known and suspected risk factors for CMPD in the external exposome, including air pollution, noise, temperature, lack of green space (or greenness), lack of blue space, grey space (or imperviousness), light-at-night, walkability, degree of urban and the social and food environment (Vlaanderen et al., 2021; Rajagopalan et al., 2024). Our objective was to develop exposure models and maps of these factors at a fine spatial resolution, at different time points, as far back as the year 2000. A key requirement was to develop harmonised maps across Europe, given that EXPANSE cohorts are spread across different parts of Europe. Because existing models and monitoring data were insufficient for our purposes, we developed new models for air pollution, temperature, road traffic noise (referred to as noise) and walkability. For green, blue and grey space, light-at-night and imperviousness, for which data or models of sufficient quality were available, we leveraged the existing remote-sensing data and land use maps to develop Europe-wide exposure surfaces.

## _2.2. External exposome factors_

## _2.2.1. Physico-chemical environment_ 

**_Air pollution_** _._ Air pollution models do exist for Europe but not at the required extent (section 2.1) nor spatial and temporal resolution. Therefore, new models were developed, building on previous work in the ELAPSE (Effects of Low-Level Air Pollution: A Study in Europe) project that created hybrid land use regression (LUR) models for Western Europe only for 2010. The ELAPSE models combined routine monitoring, satellite observations, chemical-transport modelling and fine spatial resolution traffic and land use predictors (de Hoogh et al., 2018). In EXPANSE we developed models predicting monthly and annual average concentrations (2000–2019) for ambient nitrogen dioxide (NO2), fine particles with an aerodynamic diameter of less than 2.5 µm (PM2.5), fine particles with an aerodynamic diameter of less than 10 µm (PM10) and ozone (O3) at a fine spatial scale (25x25m) (Shen et al., 2022; Shen et al., 2024). Compared to ELAPSE, we modelled individual years over a 20-year period; improved the spatial resolution (100 x 100 m to 25 x 25 m); increased spatial coverage (more countries); and allowed for variations in the relationships between predictors and concentrations across Europe and across time. 

The development of the annual models is described in detail elsewhere (Shen et al., 2022). In brief, air pollution concentrations measured at routine monitoring sites from the AIRBASE database were used to calibrate and validate the models, and a range of spatial and spatio-temporal predictor variables were used to help explain the variation in these measurements. Geographical weighted regression (GWR) outperformed supervised linear regression and random forest explaining on average 0.66, 0.77, 0.62 and 0.58 of the spatial variation (R<sup>2</sup> ) respectively for NO2, PM10, PM2.5 and O3 (five-fold hold-out-validation). Monthly models were developed to allow evaluation of medium-term exposure metrics such as seasonal averages. The development of monthly models followed a similar methodology as the annual models (Shen et al., 2024), with five-fold cross validation yielding moderate to good performances (R<sup>2</sup> : 0.31–0.66 for NO2, 0.40–0.79 for O3, 0.40–0.78 

for PM10, 0.46–0.87 for PM2.5). For the analysis presented here, we focus on the annual air pollution models as these are the main exposure metrics applied in epidemiological analyses of long-term exposure. 

**_Noise_** _._ Road traffic noise data across Europe is available through the strategic noise maps required by the Environmental Noise Directive (END). These data, however, have large spatial gaps, being mandatory only for urban agglomerations with population _>_ 100,000 and around major roads (more than 3 million vehicles per year), and historically is not fully harmonised across Europe (Khomenko et al., 2022). Also, the END requires reporting only above 55 dB Lden (50 dB Lnight), and in 5 dB intervals thus lacking estimation at lower levels. To harmonise noise modelling across our study area, the CNOSSOS-EU (Common NOise aSSessment MethOdS) framework was employed for modelling noise indicators including Laeq16, Lday, Levening, Lnight, and Lden (in dB) 

(Kephalopoulos et al., 2014). 

The implementation of CNOSSOS-EU by Morley et al. (Morley et al., 2015), applied in EXPANSE to create the EU noise map, is a simplified sound propagation model developed for Europe-wide noise mapping requiring a range of input data including a detailed road network, traffic intensity, land use and building data. Traffic intensity is a crucial input variable for road noise modelling. However, due to a lack of freely available traffic flow data across Europe, we first developed a geostatistical random forest (RF) model capable of estimating traffic flow for the year 2019 for all roads in Europe based upon traffic counts in multiple European countries (Shen et al., 2024). We subsequently modelled the following noise metrics: Lden, Lday, Lnight, Leve and Laeq16 at the maximum exposed building façade for all buildings from OpenStreetMap for the year 2019. We evaluated the performance of our EU noise model in the United Kingdom, Switzerland, the Netherlands and Stockholm (Sweden), by comparing our noise estimates with estimates from respective national noise propagation models (i.e. current gold standard). For Lden, for example, the comparison yielded moderate correlations for Switzerland, the Netherlands and the United Kingdom (Pearson’s correlation = 0.52–0.77) and a lower correlation in Stockholm County (Pearson’s correlation = 0.39) (Shen et al., 2025).

## _2.2.2. Built environment_ 

The majority of built environment data (greenness, imperviousness or grey space, artificial light-at-night, degree of urban indicator) were obtained from existing data sources and resampled to the necessary spatial and temporal resolution. The remaining factors (distance to green and blue spaces and walkability) were newly calculated within GIS. Supplement S2 gives a detailed description of the built environment data, with a short summary per variable below. 

## **_Green space_** _._ 

- Normalised difference vegetation index (NDVI) satellite imagery data were collected for the years 2000, 2005, 2010, 2015, and 2020 using MODIS product (MOD13Q1) and processed using Google Earth Engine to obtain the annual surfaces at 250 x 250 m resolution for the study area. In each year, all valid individual images were used. A raster dataset was then generated with the mean, standard deviation, and median value per pixel. The process included removing water features using a high-resolution water mask layer (Joint Research Centre Global Surface Water) (Pekel et al., 2016) and a quality filter to remove non-valid pixels. The surfaces were resampled to 100 x 100 m after which a focal statistics process was performed to create surfaces representing the mean for the surrounding 300 m, 500 m, and 1000 m (radius) around each pixel. These buffer sizes were selected to cover commonly used metrics in epidemiological studies of surrounding greenness. 

- The modified soil-adjusted vegetation index (MSAVI) was used to address some limitations of NDVI in areas with a high proportion of exposed soil surfaces, e.g. with high percentage of bare soil, scarce vegetation, or low chlorophyll content in plants. Qi et al (Qi et al., 1994) developed the MSAVI by calculating a soil brightness correction factor thus minimizing the effect of background soil. Imagery data were extracted from the same MODIS data source and years as NDVI. The same process described for NDVI was used to compute the mean for the surrounding 300 m, 500 m, and 1000 m (radius) around each pixel. 

- Distance to the nearest green space indicator was created using the main European datasets freely available in Europe: CORINE and Urban Atlas. To map publicly accessible green spaces, we combined green land cover types including green urban areas (parks) and sport and leisure facilities together with agricultural areas and forest and semi-natural areas. For each version (2000, 2006, 2012, and 2018 for CORINE and 2006, 2012, and 2018 for Urban Atlas), we created a 

100 x 100 m European surface with the Euclidean distance from each pixel centroid to the nearest green space, defined by the relevant classes from the respective datasets. 

**_Blue space_** _._ Three metrics were determined to depict exposure to blue space: 1) distance to the sea-shore, 2) distance to the nearest inland water feature, and 3) distance to the nearest blue space by taking the minimum of metric 1) and 2. All calculations were from the 100 x 100 m pixel centroid to the relevant feature. The EU-Hydro dataset was used to characterize the blue spaces indicators (COPERNICUS, 2019). Distance to blue space was calculated for one time point (2013) as we did not expect to see major changes in the spatial distribution of blue space over time. 

**_Grey space_** _._ Impervious surface maps (expressed as a percentage of soil sealing per area unit, i.e grey space) were generated, using the imperviousness degree layer from the Copernicus Land Surface Monitoring Service for the years 2000, 2005, 2010, 2015, and 2020 at a 100 x 100 m resolution. Impervious areas are characterized by the substitution of the original (semi-) natural land cover or water surface with an artificial, often impenetrable or sealed cover. Similar to greenness, a resampling analysis using a focal statistics process was performed to calculate the mean degree of imperviousness within 300 m, 500 m, and 1000 m (radius) around each pixel. 

**_Light-at-night_** _._ Light-at-night (LAN) was characterized using DMSP (Defense Meteorological Satellite Program) for the years 2000, 2005, and 2010 and VIIRS (Version 1 VIIRS Day/Night Band Night-time Lights) for the years 2015 and 2020. Following the previously described computation procedures, a focal statistics process was applied to compute averages within 300 m, 500 m, and 1000 m (radius) around each pixel. 

**_Walkability_** _._ A walkability index, a tool to assess how walkable neighbourhoods or cities are, was created and customised for Europe following the approach of previous indexes developed specifically for the US (Frank et al., 2010) and more recently for the Netherlands (Lam et al., 2022). These studies identified key predictor variables which were found to correlate with walking, of which the following were included in our index: street connectivity density, slope, population density, pedestrian street density, accessibility to public transport, food retail density, NDVI and distance to green space. For each indicator we calculated 100 x 100 m resolution surfaces with the average value within 300 m, 500 m, and 1000 m (radius) using focal statistics. Global population density was obtained from the 250 x 250 m dataset for 2015 (Schiavina et al., 2023). Slope was derived from the EU-DEM dataset at 25 x 25 m resolution. We used Open Street Map (OSM) to retrieve the street network and derive the connectivity and pedestrian variables. For connectivity, we retrieved the street intersection nodes from the street network and then rasterized them to create a 100 x 100 m surface with intersection density per pixel, as a connectivity indicator. For pedestrian street density, we first selected the relevant street categories (see Supplement S2) and then rasterized each street category into 10 x 10 m binary rasters (1 if a pixel contained a street, 0 otherwise). We then applied a resample method to obtain 100 x 100 m surfaces with the aggregated number of streets for each pixel. Similarly, we extracted the public transport stations for metro, bus, train and tram and derived the related surfaces with the station density per 100 x 100 m pixel. Food retail density was also obtained from OSM to produce a 100 x 100 m surface with the average value within 800 m around each pixel. We conducted several exploratory analyses to test each indicator’s completeness and robustness, and performed correlation analysis between indicators at different scales (i.e. city, country, Europe) and different urban configurations (i.e. rural vs. urban). We also performed a comparison against an existing walkability index finding moderate

**_Degree of urban_** _._ A degree of urban indicator was created based on the Global Human Settlement (GHS) population grid from the Global Human Settlement Layer (GHSL) (Schiavina et al., 2022). A focal mean within 1500 m (square window) was applied to create a 100 x 100 mm population density surface. The degree of urban indicator was calculated on a continuous scale, with low to high scores indicating rural to urban. We did not set a threshold for differentiating when a pixel is either urban or rural, as this depends on the local setting and is therefore potentially different across Europe. 

## _2.3. Food environment_ 

No European datasets characterizing the geographical food environment (e.g., availability food retailers within an area) existed in 2020. We therefore set out to construct the following indicators using Open Street Map (OSM) data (Pinho et al., 2023). We extracted points of interest (POIs) from OSM labelled as fast-food outlets, supermarkets and restaurants and separately calculated the counts within 800 m buffers around randomly selected residential addresses. We tested the quality of the OSM data in five European regions (the Randstad (Netherlands), Barcelona (Spain), Basel (Switzerland), Greater Athens (Greece) and Lodz (Poland)) by comparing them with Google Street View (GSV) im-ˆ ages and found that although the agreement was poor for individual points of interest, OSM data were able to broadly explain the spatial variation of the food environment using the count of selected food retailers as indicator in three of the five regions (i.e. Randstad, Barcelona and Basel) (Pinho et al., 2023). As we could not validate other regions we do not support using OSM data beyond the Randstad, Basel, and Barcelona at this time. Also, in these three regions, only restaurant density, fast food restaurant density and supermarket density were deemed of sufficient quality. 

## _2.4. Social environment_ 

Harmonised data characterising the social environment in which individuals live is not currently available at a meaningful small area or neighbourhood European-wide level. By social environment, we refer to a number of dimensions including the material, socio-economic and socio-demographic characteristics of the neighbourhood. This includes the social composition of the neighbourhood (average socio-economic level (education, income, occupation), proportion of single-parent families, immigrants), the crime rate, the safety of the neighbourhood and the availability of public services (e.g. schools). In EXPANSE we evaluated European Union Statistics on Income and Living Conditions (EUSILC) data for harmonisation of social factors across Europe and identified other data sources with social variables reflecting the living conditions of people in their neighbourhood. However, we found a lack of consistent, publicly available social data at the neighbourhood level. 

Due to these limitations, it was not feasible within the project timeframe to develop a harmonized area level socioeconomic status (SES) and/or deprivation variable for Europe. 

## _2.5. Statistical analyses_ 

## _2.5.1. Geographical level of analysis_ 

To investigate the relationships between exposures and their stability over time, we generated a random dataset of 120,264 building facades points across Europe (Fig. S1). The selection was stratified by population density within NUTS1 region (First-level Classification of Territorial Units for Statistics), using the 100x100 m population estimates for year 2020 from the GHSL (Schiavina et al., 2023). These random points represented a stratified selection of the location of the European population and was used in that capacity to investigate the correlation between and the spatial and temporal stability of the exposures. 

## _2.5.2. Correlations between exposures_ 

The correlation between all the different exposures was investigated for the most recent year of each, using: 2019 for the four air pollutants (NO2, PM2.5, PM10 and O3), temperature and noise; and 2020 for NDVI, MSAVI, imperviousness, light-at-night, and degree of urban. Exposures were extracted at the random points and the following correlations were calculated: Pearson’s R at both the European and country level, and Spearman’s rank correlation at the European level. The correlation matrix was visualized using a correlogram. The analysis at the European level could obscure differences in exposure both between and within countries. Thus with the analysis at the country level, we wanted to assess how exposures are correlated within countries. This is important as most health studies do not exploit European-wide exposure contrasts (Strak et al., 2021; Stafoggia et al., 2022), but are analyzed at a national or sub-national level. The analysis also addressed heterogeneity of within-country correlations. For the purpose of this assessment, we defined Pearson’s correlation coefficients (R) as weak (R _<_ 0.4), moderate ( _>_ 0.4–0.6), moderately strong ( _>_ 0.6–0.8), strong ( _>_ 0.8–0.9) and very strong ( _>_ 0.9). 

## _2.5.3. Stability in time_ 

Stability of the spatial structure for exposure surfaces was explored for the four air pollutants (NO2, PM2.5, PM10 and O3), temperature, NDVI, MSAVI, imperviousness and light-at-night by comparing two time points. These exposures were extracted at the random points, and correlations calculated for 2000 vs. 2019/20 and 2015 vs. 2019/20 at the European and country level. We reported Pearson’s R. For the air pollutants and temperature we also assessed stability of the exposure surfaces for intermediate time points. 

## _2.5.4. Trends in exposures over time_ 

To evaluate the time trends, we used the whole raster datasets, not the 120,000 random points. ## _3.2. Stability of exposure over time_ 

Correlations at the ~ 120,000 random points across Europe revealed robust stability at the European level for most factors with temporally resolved surfaces (Fig. 4 and Fig. S8). Strong to very strong correlations were observed between early (2000) and late years (2020 – 2019) for all factors at the European level, except of O3, which correlated moderately strong (R 0.66). For example, O3 and PM10 in Slovakia showed weak correlations between 2000 and 2019 (R 0.39 and 0.41 respectively) and PM2.5 in the Netherlands also showed a weaker correlation (R 0.45) (Fig. 4 and Fig. S8).

## _4.2. Stability over time_ 

Previous studies have documented stability of measured and modelled air pollution surfaces over periods of at least 10 years in Europe and North America (Eeftens et al., 2011; de Hoogh et al., 2018; Cesaroni et al., 2012; Gulliver et al., 2013). de Hoogh et al. (de Hoogh et al., 2019) evaluated the spatial structure of modelled air pollution surfaces using Hybrid/LUR method for NO2 (2010 with 2005 and 2000) and PM2.5 (2010 with 2013). They found very strong correlations between the NO2 surface for 2010 and the 2005 and 2000 NO2 surfaces 

(R2 _>_ 0.91) across the whole of Europe and between the 2010 and 2013 PM2.5 surfaces (R2 = 0.88). These are very similar as our findings (NO2 R2 = 0.89; PM2.5: R2 = 0.80 comparing 2000 – 2019). Our study documents very high correlations for a period of 20 years in a wide range of European countries. 

Our results show robust stability of spatial contrast for most environment factors for which we had exposure data for multiple time points. Few other studies have evaluated this issue beyond air pollution. Our findings for green space are in line with an analysis within the pooled cohort of the ELAPSE study (Bereziartua et al., 2022) and studies in Barcelona and Canada (Nieuwenhuijsen et al., 2018; Villeneuve et al., 2012).The spatial stability is explained by the relative stability in the location of dominant sources (major roads, industrial area) in the study area between 2000 and 2020. In areas with strong infrastructural developments or change in industrial activity, spatial patterns may be less stable.

## _4.3. Trends in exposure over time_ 

A comprehensive analysis of time trends in air pollution concentrations in rural environments across Europe was conducted by Aas et al. (Aas et al., 2024) between 2000 and 2019 using measured data at rural monitoring stations. They reported annual reductions of 2.4 % for PM2.5 and 1.7 % for NO2. Chen et al. (Chen et al., 2024) reported trends for NO2, O3, PM10 and PM2.5 between 2000 and 2019 using measured data at background stations reporting annual reductions of 2.4 % (NO2), 2.3 % (PM2.5) and 2.7 % (PM10). For O3 an increase of 0.2 % was found. Our data shows similar annual changes (reductions of 1.3 % for NO2, 1.8 % for PM2.5 and PM10 and an increase of 0.4 % for O3) noting that our data is using a population weighted mean compared to a rural environment mean used by Aas et al. (Aas et al., 2024). 

We observe a modest increase of 0.6<sup>◦</sup> C across Europe between 2005 and 2020. However, when looking at the country-level, the change varies more, between − 0.1 and 2.3<sup>◦</sup> C. The increases are in line with an observed temperature increase globally which is attributed to global warming (IPCC IPoCC. Climate Change, 2021). 

A study of Verhoeven and Dedoussi (Verhoeven and Dedoussi, 2022) assessed the trend across Europe in NDVI-derived land cover classes between 2000 and 2019 and observed that forest land cover increased, whilst agricultural and shrubs and herbaceous vegetation showed the opposite trend. We observed a modest increase in NDVI and MSAVI across Europe, showing some heterogeneity at the country level. We were not able to distinguish trends between different green land cover classes. 

A study by Bennie et al. (Bennie et al., 2014) showed that although night time light pollution in Europe between 1995 and 2010 overall increased, some countries, including Sweden, Denmark and Belgium, contain areas where a decline was detected. In our assessment we observe a decrease in light-at-night for the same countries. 

## **_Implications_** 

For most of the exposure factors we assessed, we show that trends in time follow the same direction (e.g. decrease in air pollution, increase in temperature, increase in greenness and imperviousness surfaces) at both the European and the national scale. However, the magnitude of change in population weighted exposures per country (Fig. 6 and Table S2) differs between time periods. 

## _4.4. Limitations_ 

We were not able to develop harmonised exposure data for the food and social environment. Sufficient quality OSM data to characterize the food environment was only available for the three regions we were able to evaluate (Randstad in the Netherlands, Barcelona and Basel). More work needs to be done to improve the OSM data in regard to identifying and updating these types of food environment, and other data sources, like Google or Bing, could be harnessed. There are datasets available that depict the food environment at the local scale, such as the commercial LOCATUS database in the Netherlands or the General Classification of Economic Activities (NOGA) in Switzerland (Pinho et al., 2023), and potential local administrative data sources such as business registries. Mapping the social environment, using area level socioeconomic position or deprivation indexes, on a small/reasonable geographical scale has proved too complex on a European scale. Social data, such as education, employment and income, is very widely held and accessible within countries, most often in the national language of the country concerned, which makes it difficult to access. In addition, the social data collected between countries is not the same and is aggregated on very different spatial scales. All this hampers the 

development of a harmonised index of socieconomic position or deprivation at European level. There are initiatives to develop harmonised indices of disadvantage in Europe, but this only concerns a few European countries. There is a need to facilitate access to social data across Europe on a small spatial scale, and to work towards harmonising key factors or deriving social factors from pan-European data sources. 

This was the first pan-European noise modeling effort aimed at producing harmonised exposures. However, we were not able to conduct a historical exposure assessment for noise, due to the lack of historical input data specifically road data and traffic volumes. Vienneau et al. (Vienneau et al., 2024), however documented high correlations (R 0.91) in modelled road traffic noise levels between 2001 and 2010 at residential locations in Switzerland, thereby indicating that noise may be stable over time as well. 

A study by Huss et al. (Huss et al., 2019) found that satellite-based measurements of light-at-night did not correlate with indoor bedroom illuminance levels, suggesting that satellite data may not accurately reflect personal indoor light exposure during evening and nighttime hours. In contrast, for other environmental exposures such as air pollution, noise, and heat, significant relationships between outdoor and indoor levels have been documented (Foraster et al., 2014; Meier et al., 2015; Nguyen et al., 2014). However, factors like building characteristics and individual activity patterns can influence these relationships, potentially leading to exposure misclassification. 

The external exposome encompasses a wide range of exposures, such as pesticides and other chemicals, microplastics, ultrafine particulates, ionizing and non-ionizing radiation, which we were unable to assess during this project. The primary reason for this was the lack of harmonized European-wide data necessary for modelling these exposures at the required fine spatial and temporal scale. Future efforts should prioritize extending the number of pan-European exposure surfaces for key determinants. The potential benefit of optimizing existing models may be smaller compared to the gains from focusing on new exposures. This importantly points to the need for harmonized exposure assessment from the start. 

Observed correlations between modelled exposures were not only driven by the exposures themselves but also by the underlying model structures as they 1) share similar predictor variables or 2) in some models they serve as input information to estimate other exposures. 

## _4.5. Utility of EXPANSE data_ 

Harmonised spatial and time-varying pan European data on environmental stressors is important as to: 

1) **study exposure patterns in time and space across Europe** ; We showed patterns over time and between countries. This data can be used to identify where interventions should take place. From an epidemiological perspective, the fact that previous analyses did not use timevarying exposures may not have been a major issue _per se_ , though it would influence the estimated risk per unit of exposure. Trend analyses showed that, although some environmental conditions appear to be improving, this improvement is not consistent across all regions of Europe. Specifically, the Balkans seemed to experience slower improvement, and in some cases, environmental conditions were deteriorating. 

2 **) investigate the interrelationship between environmental factors** ; overall, the correlations were weak, which allows the application of most multivariable exposure-health models. However, some correlations were influenced by underlying data structures, which should be acknowledged when interpreting these models. Additionally, correlations varied between countries and when pooled across Europe, complicating the interpretation of Europe-wide analyses, especially when the direction of the correlation changed. For example, a positive correlation may appear across Europe due to country-level differences, but within most countries, the same factors may be negatively correlated.

## **5. Conclusions** 

Data will be made available on request. 

To our knowledge, this is one of the most comprehensive analyses of the temporal and spatial relationships between external exposome factors across a large geographical area. Most factors show low to moderate correlations, supporting the need for multi-exposure analyses in exposure health analyses. 

We found substantial spatial stability of exposure surfaces over time across Europe, supporting that modest temporal misalignment between exposure model and follow-up period of the health study is not critical. Also, that we did not model every year for the environmental factors known to change less over time, is not likely to be problematic. We also showed that air pollutant levels have declined while temperatures have risen over the study period, which should be considered in epidemiological analyses. 

The data described in this paper are available to researchers via the Exposome maps platform (https://exposome.nl/tools/exposome-maps. html).
