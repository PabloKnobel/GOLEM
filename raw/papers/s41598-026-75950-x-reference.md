# **The association between short-term nitrogen dioxide (NO2) exposure and ischemic stroke or transient ischemic attacks (TIA): a national study in Israel** 

## **Placidus S. Fernando · Helena Krasnov · Itai Kloog · Mike Z. He · Gal Ifergane · Inbar Zucker · Victor Novack · Carmit Libruder · Maayan Yitshak-Sade** 

Received: 13 February 2026 / Accepted: 9 October 2026 © The Author(s) 2026 

#### **Abstract** 

Air pollution is an environmental risk factor for stroke, but most studies have focused on fine particulate matter (PM2.5), and fewer have investigated the independent association with exposure to nitrogen dioxide (NO2). We conducted a nationwide case-crossover study including all ischemic stroke or transient ischemic attack (TIA) reported in the Israeli National Stroke Registry from 2014 to 2018. We obtained daily NO2 levels from high-resolution geospatial models and linked them to residential addresses. We used distributed lag nonlinear models to assess the cumulative association of NO2 exposure over 0–5 lag days, adjusting for PM2.5 and temperature. We further evaluated the modification of the associations by age, sex, urbanicity, and comorbidities (ischemic heart disease, hypertension, and diabetes). We included 85,162 cases, about 54% were males, and the mean age was 71.8. Cumulative NO2 exposure was associated with increased odds of ischemic stroke or TIA (odds ratio – OR and 95% confidence interval − 1.057 [1.034–1.081]), with the strongest effects 3–5 days before the event. The associations were more pronounced in urban areas (1.064 [1.032;1.097]) compared to suburban areas (1.045 [1.012;1.080]). Although the overall association was similar across ages and sexes, the lag-specific associations on lag days 3–5 were more pronounced in younger adults and women. Short-term exposure to NO2 is associated with an increased risk of ischemic stroke and TIA, with differential associations across urbanicity, sex, and age groups. These results underscore the urgent need for public health initiatives to reduce NO2 emissions.

## **Introduction** 

Stroke is one of the leading causes of death and disability in the world<sup>1</sup> . Each year, there are over 12 million new stroke cases worldwide, an increase of more than 50% over the last two decades<sup>2</sup> . Therefore, research in recent decades has focused on identifying modifiable risk factors that can be targeted to reduce the global stroke burden<sup>3</sup> . There is substantial evidence indicating that exposure to air pollution increases the risk of cardiovascular diseases (CVD) beyond traditional CVD risk factors<sup>4</sup> . Given that most of the global population currently lives in regions where air pollution levels exceed the World Health Organization’s Global Air Quality Guidelines, air pollution is a critical target for stroke prevention<sup>5,6</sup> . 

Most existing research has focused on particulate matter smaller than 2.5 microns (PM2.5), with studies reporting increased stroke risk associated with both long-term (1.01 per 10 µg/m<sup>3</sup> ; 95% CI 1.011–1.012) and short-term exposures (1.06 per 10 µg/m<sup>3</sup> 95% CI 1.018–1.105)<sup>6,7</sup> . Although nitrogen dioxide (NO2) has been studied, the evidence is less extensive than for PM2.5, and important research gaps remain<sup>8,9</sup> . These gaps underscore the need to further investigate the health effects associated with NO2 exposure<sup>6</sup> . PM2.5 and NO2 arise from different emission sources accompanied by distinct mixtures of co-occurring environmental stressors. Investigating the effects of NO2 independently of PM2.5 is therefore of particular importance from a policy standpoint, enabling more targeted air quality regulation aimed at specific sources of hazardous exposure. 

NO2 is an anthropogenic air pollutant originating primarily from traffic and industrial sources through the combustion of fossil fuels<sup>10</sup> . Studies suggest that NO2 may increase stroke risk by triggering oxidative stress, systemic inflammation, and endothelial dysfunction, leading to blood clots and restricted blood flow to the brain<sup>11</sup> . While NO2 shares biological pathways similar to other pollutants, such as PM2.5, its strong correlation with traffic-related patterns may reflect distinct exposure patterns. A large meta-analysis in 2023 found that NO2 exposure is associated with stroke hospitalizations and mortality (1.014 per 10 ppb, 95% CI 1.009–1.019)<sup>6</sup> . However, the analysis concluded that there was insufficient evidence to establish a causal relationship between exposure to NO2 and stroke, citing limitations in the quantity, quality, consistency, and statistical power of the available studies<sup>6</sup> . These limitations likely reflect heterogeneity across study designs and populations, limited geographic representation, residual confounding by co-pollutants, and relatively coarse exposure assessment methods. 

To address some of these limitations, we leverage the Israeli National Stroke Registry (INSR) data and highresolution spatiotemporal NO2 exposure models to investigate the association between short-term NO2 exposure and ischemic stroke or transient ischemic attacks (TIA). We further examine potential effect modification by urbanicity, demographic, and clinical characteristics. Israel’s unique topography, ranging from coastal lowlands to mountainous regions and deserts, creates heterogeneous environmental conditions that may influence air pollution exposure patterns, thereby enhancing the generalizability of our findings<sup>12</sup> .

## **Methods** 

### **Population and outcome** 

We used data from the INSR, which includes all permanent residents in Israel admitted to general hospitals with a primary diagnosis of stroke or TIA, reported using the International Classification of Diseases, ninth edition (ICD-9). We included all ischemic stroke (ICD-9: 433, 434.x1, 436, or 997.02) and TIA (ICD-9: 435) cases among individuals 18 years and older across Israel between 2014 and 2018. The INSR data are retrieved from electronic medical records and include diagnoses and comorbidities coded using ICD-9, encompassing hospitalization diagnoses, chronic conditions, and discharge diagnoses. Sociodemographic information (age, sex, ethnicity, and geocoded address) is retrieved from the Ministry of Interior database and linked to the INSR. Ethnicity was grouped into Jewish and non-Jewish due to the small proportion of non-Jewish individuals (Arab, Druze, or other ethnic groups). 

This study was approved by the institutional review boards at Soroka University Medical Center in Beer Sheva, Israel (study 01718-19), and the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai, New York, USA (study 21-00076). A waiver of informed consent was granted due to the minimal risk nature of the study and the use of de-identified registry data with no direct participant contact. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant institutional and national guidelines and regulations. 

### **Exposure** 

We obtained daily 1-km<sup>2</sup> predictions of NO2 from a hybrid spatiotemporal model we built over Israel<sup>13</sup> . The model, which is based on the methodology we previously used for an NO2 model in Mexico City, combines satellite, land-use, and meteorological data and employs machine-learning methods (random forest and gradient boosting) for prediction<sup>14</sup> . The model was rigorously evaluated using 10-fold cross-validation and demonstrated strong performance with an R<sup>2</sup> of 0.88 and a root mean square error of 3.81 µg/m<sup>3</sup> . The exposures were then linked to each individual based on the proximity of the geocoded residential address to the corresponding 1-km² grid cells of the satellite-based model predictions. We excluded 1,289 individuals without a valid residential address (1.6%). 

### **Covariates** 

Models were adjusted for temperature, and fine particulate matter (PM2.5) averaged over the same exposure window as NO2. We obtained daily mean PM2.5 and temperature estimates from satellite-based spatiotemporal models that use a hybrid approach to estimate daily mean temperature and PM2.5 exposures at a fine resolution<sup>15,16</sup> . Briefly, these models use both extreme gradient boosting and inverse-distance weighting techniques, incorporating aerosol optical depth, meteorological, and land-use variables to generate daily predictions at 1-km resolution. The models were calibrated daily using a mixed-model approach, with MODIS-based multiangle implementation of atmospheric correction (MAIAC) aerosol optic depth (AOD) and various spatial and temporal predictors. This resulted in excellent performance with out-of-sample cross-validated R<sup>2</sup> values of 0.82–0.92 for the PM2.5 model and 0.96–0.98 for the temperature model. 

### **Statistical analysis** 

We used distributed lag nonlinear models (DLNMs) in a case-crossover design to investigate the associations between NO2 exposure and ischemic stroke or TIA. This approach accounts for confounders that do not vary within a short period by design and allows the flexible estimation of lag-specific and cumulative associations over time<sup>17</sup> . We defined the admission day as the case day, and matched control days by the day of the week within

the same month and year. We defined the exposure window as exposure on lag days 0–5, based on the smallest Bayesian Information Criteria (BIC). We used a linear function for the exposure response association, which was confirmed upon visual inspection, and natural splines with two degrees of freedom (df) to estimate the lag structures, based on the smallest BIC. Then, we used generalized nonlinear models (GNM) with conditional stratification. We adjusted the models for the temperature mean for lags 0–4. The lag window for temperature was selected based on model fit to minimize residual confounding from delayed temperature exposure. 

We conducted an interaction analysis to evaluate whether the cumulative association between NO₂ exposure and ischemic stroke/TIA differed by urbanicity (cities with over 100,000 residents versus smaller cities, towns, and villages), comorbidities (diabetes, hypertension, and ischemic heart disease [IHD]), sex, and age. We classified individuals as younger than 55 years versus 55 years and older, a cutoff commonly used to define stroke in young adults<sup>18</sup> . We repeated the models separately for each potential effect modifier, adding an interaction term between the NO₂ cross-basis and each subgroup indicator. Because the exposure-response is represented by multiple spline and lag parameters, the interaction produces a set of coefficients rather than a single term. We therefore used the likelihood ratio test (LRT) to assess whether all interaction parameters significantly differ from the null, comparing models with and without the interaction term. A statistically significant LRT p-value indicates evidence of differences in the _shape_ or _timing_ of the lagged response across groups, even if the estimated cumulative risks over the full lag period appear similar. For interpretability, we present group-specific cumulative ORs and 95% confidence intervals (Cis) and provide lag-specific effects for subgroups with statistically significant modifiers. 

### **Sensitivity analyses** 

We conducted several sensitivity analyses to ensure the robustness of our findings. First, we repeated the main model using alternative definitions of cumulative NO₂ exposure, summing exposure over 3-, 4-, 5-, and 6-day lag windows. Second, we repeated the main models with ischemic stroke only, excluding TIA, to ensure that the associations with NO2 did not differ between groups. Third, since baseline stroke risk may differ between firstoccurrence and recurrent stroke events, we reran the models excluding recurrent events to ensure the association did not change. Finally, we further adjusted the models for PM2.5 exposure on the day of the event to ensure our results were not confounded by it. 

## **Results** 

We included 85,162 cases, of whom 69.9% had ischemic stroke and 30.1% had TIA. About 54% were males, 80.9% were Jewish, 50.28% resided in large cities, and the mean age was 71.8 years. Cardiometabolic diseases were highly prevalent in this population, with 45.8% presenting diabetes, about 15% CHF, and 77.9% hypertension. Finally, about 3% of the cases had a history of myocardial infarction, and 13.64% had recurring stroke events (Table 1). 

The largest number of cases was observed in the summer season (33.42%). Overall, the mean NO2 exposure during the observation period was 12.34 ppb, with higher levels in large cities (13.62 ppb) than in suburban and rural areas (11.02 ppb). The mean PM2.5 exposure level was 19.04 µg/m<sup>3</sup> , with similar levels between large cities (19.28 µg/m<sup>3</sup> ) and suburban and rural areas (18.79 µg/m<sup>3</sup> ). The mean temperature during the observation period was 21.40 °C (Table 2). The correlation between air pollutants and temperature was low, with the highest correlations between NO2 and temperature ( _r_ = − 0.2) and between NO2 and PM2.5 ( _r_ = 0.13). A very low correlation was observed between temperature and PM2.5 ( _r_ = 0.05). 

The cumulative OR for ischemic stroke or TIA associated with an 8 ppb (standard deviation [SD]) increase in NO₂ exposure, summed over lag days 0–5, was 1.057 (95% confidence interval [CI] 1.034, 1.081) (Fig. 1A). ## **Discussion** 

In this nationwide study encompassing diverse populations and climate regions, we observed increased odds of ischemic stroke or TIA associated with short-term NO2 exposure. Disentangling the cumulative association, we observed that the risk of ischemic stroke and TIA peaked 3–5 days after NO2 exposure. This pattern is consistent with studies suggesting that exposure to air pollution can trigger pulmonary and systemic inflammation, procoagulant changes, and vascular dysfunction, which typically evolve over several days after exposure<sup>11,19</sup> . 

Further, our findings revealed statistically significant interactions among urbanicity, sex, and age. While cumulative associations were broadly similar between men and women, and overall larger among older adults and urban residents, in lag-specific analyses, women, younger adults, and rural and suburban residents all exhibited a

steeper, monotonically increasing lag structure; transitioning from inverse associations at early lags to markedly stronger positive associations at lags 3–5, compared to their respective reference groups. 

While most of the existing literature on air pollution and stroke has focused on PM2.5, a few studies have demonstrated an association between short-term NO₂ exposure and an increased risk of ischemic stroke, supporting our findings<sup>9,20</sup> . Furthermore, a 2023 meta-analysis reported a 1.4% increase in stroke mortality and hospitalizations per 10 ppb rise in NO₂<sup>6</sup> . Unlike NO2, PM2.5 can originate from sources beyond traffic, including natural atmospheric reactions, making it a broader marker of various emission sources<sup>21</sup> . NO2, however, is a marker of traffic-related air pollution, representing a complex exposure that encompasses gaseous toxicity and noise<sup>22,23</sup> . Using the same Israeli stroke registry data, a prior study found elevated ischemic stroke risk associated with PM2.5 exposure below the national threshold<sup>24</sup> , and our current findings extend this evidence by demonstrating that NO2 exposure is independently associated with increased odds of stroke after adjustment for PM2.5 and temperature. The differential associations observed across urbanicity strata further emphasize the importance of investigating pollutant-specific effects while accounting for co-exposures. Understanding the independent contribution of NO₂ to stroke risk is therefore essential for informing targeted air quality regulations and traffic management policies aimed at reducing the cerebrovascular burden of traffic-related air pollution. 

A key finding of our study is the differential susceptibility observed across population groups. We found evidence of effect modification by urbanicity, sex, and age, with somewhat stronger cumulative associations among residents of large cities and older adults. These patterns are consistent with existing epidemiological studies showing that individuals in certain sociodemographic groups, including older adults, may be more susceptible to the adverse effects of air pollution<sup>19</sup> . Furthermore, the stronger associations in urban areas may be attributed to heightened exposure intensity, co-exposures (e.g., temperature, noise), as well as socioeconomic and environmental factors that may amplify the NO2 effect<sup>16,25</sup> . 

Notably, the investigation of the lag-specific patterns revealed a more complex interaction structure. Women, younger adults, and rural and suburban residents all exhibited more delayed but sharper biological responses to NO₂ exposure. The comparison groups exhibited more modest, but positive lag-specific associations throughout most of the exposure window. The delayed effects in certain population groups may be related to lower baseline risk, intervening biological factors<sup>19,26</sup> , or differential exposure profiles<sup>27</sup> . The divergence between cumulative and lag-specific effects underscores the importance of examining the full temporal distribution of risk rather than relying solely on summary measures, as cumulative estimates may mask heterogeneity in the timing and magnitude of acute responses across population subgroups<sup>20</sup> . 

Strengths of our study include high spatiotemporal resolution in exposure assessment, a population-based design, and rich outcome and covariate data. The case-crossover design inherently accounts for time-invariant confounders. However, some limitations remain. First, residual confounding from concurrent environmental exposures is possible, though adjustment for PM2.5 and temperature did not meaningfully alter the results. Second, NO₂ exposure was estimated based on residential addresses, without accounting for time spent at workplaces, in transit, or at other locations. Since individuals may spend a portion of their day outside the home, this approach may not fully capture personal exposure during the study period. However, residence-based exposure assessment is the predominant approach in large-scale epidemiological studies of air pollution and health, and several validation studies have demonstrated that address-based and time-activity-based exposures yield similar health effects<sup>28</sup> . In the context of a case-crossover design, where each participant serves as their own control, any exposure misclassification is unlikely to differ systematically between the case and control periods, leading to non-differential exposure misclassification, which typically biases effect estimates toward the null<sup>29</sup> . Finally, given the relatively high mortality observed in our data, our sample may over-represent more severe cases, limiting the generalizability of our findings to milder cerebrovascular events. If NO2 is more strongly associated with severe strokes, our results may overestimate the association in the overall stroke population. Since stroke severity data were not available, we were unable to assess this possibility. 

In conclusion, this nationwide study demonstrates that short-term NO₂ exposure is independently associated with increased odds of ischemic stroke and TIA, with effects that vary across sex, age, and urbanicity. Lag-specific
