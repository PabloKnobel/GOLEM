###### A B S T R A C T 

An increasing number of studies have examined the effects of green prescription on various health outcomes. The aim of this study was to systematically review published randomized controlled trial studies on the health benefits of green prescriptions. We searched PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science for the relevant original articles published in English until June 30, 2023. Our search initially retrieved 26176 articles, of which 31 studies were included in our review after removing the duplicates and excluding ineligible articles based on their titles, abstracts, and full-text review. Consistent positive health benefits of green prescription were reported for psychological health and wellbeing (16 out of 24 studies), cardiometabolic health (five out of nine studies), physical activity (eight out of nine studies), and inflammation (two out of two studies). The reviewed studies did not report any significant benefits in orthopedic conditions, pain, and recovery from exhaustion disorder due to their implemented green prescriptions; however, the number of studies was too small to generalize the effect of green prescriptions on these outcomes. The quality of these studies was generally acceptable, with 28 studies having some concerns regarding their overall risk of bias and only three studies with a high risk of bias. All in all, this systematic review suggests that green prescriptions can be effective in improving various health outcomes, but further studies with larger sample sizes and objective measures are needed. 

##### **1. Introduction** 

Today’s urban areas house more than half of the world’s population (Ritchie and Roser, 2018). Population growth, a lack of resources, and the growing effects of climate change are putting a greater strain on many urban areas (WHO, 2017), leaving residents with little or no access to green spaces. As a result, there is an urgent need for novel strategies to improve the health and wellbeing of urban residents in the face of global environmental changes, promote sustainable lifestyles, and raise the quality of urban settings. 

Natural environments, including green spaces, offer opportunities to improve the quality and resilience of urban settings, as well as the health and wellbeing of urban residents (WHO, 2017). Interaction with green spaces offers a variety of mental, physical, and social benefits for humans, including stress reduction, reduced risk of psychological 

problems such as anxiety and depression (Triguero-Mas et al., 2015), improved pregnancy outcomes (Glazer et al., 2018) and child health and development (Liao et al., 2019), reduced risk of morbidity and mortality in adults (Kondo et al., 2018); enhanced healthy aging (Barton and Pineo, 2009), increased social cohesion (Jennings and Bamkole, 2019) and reduced health inequality (Mitchell and Popham, 2008). 

These insights have sparked a flurry of initiatives to (re)connect people with nature, particularly those unable to engage in green space activities regularly. Many of these initiatives have focused on “bringing nature to people” by greening places in areas such as neighborhoods, schoolyards, urban public spaces, hospitals, classrooms, and offices (Wolch et al., 2014; Van den Berg et al., 2016). Other initiatives have been aimed at “bringing people to nature” by encouraging and facilitating the active participation of children and adults in nature-based activities (Maas et al., 2009), which includes, among others, green

Green prescription is a nature-based health intervention encompassing activities involving spending time and engaging with natural environments to benefit human health and well-being (Robinson and Breed, 2019). It includes activities like green gyms, population-based health promotion programs, horticultural therapy for people with specific needs, and more therapeutic interventions like care farms, walk-and-talk coaching, forest bathing, and horticultural therapy (Van den Berg, 2017). 

Over the past decade, an increasing number of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have evaluated the effects of green prescriptions on various health outcomes. Like any other intervention, the large-scale rollout of green prescriptions in society requires a synthesized evidence base to inform practitioners and policymakers about the effectiveness of these interventions and enable them to develop evidence-based policies and guidelines. A number of review studies have investigated the impact of nature-based interventions on health outcomes (Roberts et al., 2020; Harper et al., 2021; Kondo et al., 2020; Nguyen et al., 2023). However, the majority of these studies were broad in scope and did not specifically focus on green prescriptions. Furthermore, other studies did not exclusively rely on randomized controlled trials (RCTs) as their primary evidence base. Green prescription relies on the active involvement of clinicians who play a crucial role in prescribing nature-based interventions to their patients. However, persuading clinicians about the effectiveness of green prescription interventions can be challenging, especially when evidence is derived from various epidemiological study designs. Considering this challenge, the objective of this study was to conduct a systematic review that focuses explicitly on RCT studies to examine the health benefits associated with green prescriptions thoroughly. 

##### _2.3. Data extraction_ 

The following data were extracted from each study using predesigned data extraction forms: first author, date of publication, study location, type of intervention, comparison group, sample size, participants’ characteristics, reported outcomes, outcome assessment methods, study duration, and summary of results. 

##### _2.4. Data synthesis_ 

We used narrative synthesis to integrate the findings into descriptive summaries regarding the health benefits of green prescriptions. Metaanalysis was not used for synthesis because of a lack of comparable quantitative outcomes across studies, as well as differences in implementation and outcome measurement tools. 

##### _2.5. Risk of bias assessment_ 

The revised Cochrane’s risk of bias tool (Higgins et al., 2021) (ROB 2.0) was used to assess the risk of bias in each study included in the review. Accordingly, we assessed the quality of each study under five bias domains: bias arising from the randomization process; bias due to deviations from intended interventions; bias due to missing outcome data; bias in the measurement of the outcome; and bias in the selection of reported results. The judgments about the risk of bias for each domain assessed were derived by a computer-generated algorithm embedded in the tool, based on answers to specific signaling questions present in each of the bias domains, and as such were graded “low risk”, “some concerns”, or “high risk”. The tool’s algorithm generated the overall risk of bias for each study based on the combination of the judgments from each bias domain assessed (Higgins et al., 2021).

##### _2.1. Search strategy_ 

We conducted this systematic review following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. We identified articles using the keywords for three main domains: green space exposure, prescription, and randomized control trials. The Boolean operator “OR” was used between the keywords in each domain and the “AND” operator between the three domains. In each of the databases, the search term – excluding the syntax – was the following: (greenspace OR “green space” OR green* OR “urban natural environment” OR forest* OR garden* OR park* OR “Built environment” OR “Urban design” OR “blue space” OR natur* OR “natural areas” OR “open spaces” OR landscape OR “nature-based intervention”) AND (prescription OR recommendation OR therapy OR prescribing OR advice OR guidance OR intervention) AND (RCT OR “randomized controlled trial” OR “controlled clinical trial” OR “controlled trial”). As such, selected articles included at least one keyword from each domain. Search terms and the syntax used to identify relevant studies for the review in each database are presented in Table S1. 

##### _2.2. Study identification and selection_ 

We searched PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science to identify (i) peer-reviewed original articles reporting on (ii) RCTs assessing the health benefits of green prescriptions on humans and (iii) published in English until June 30, 2023. After removing the duplicates, subsequent rounds of exclusion included reading the titles, abstracts, and final articles. To find relevant articles, we also searched the reference list of the retrieved articles. The review was carried out with the help of Rayyan (https://rayyan.ai), an online tool made specifically to facilitate literature reviews. 

##### _3.1. Study identification and selection_ 

The initial database search yielded 26176 records, including 15,990 records from Scopus, 7961 records from the Web of Science, and 2225 articles from PubMed, of which 10,263 were duplicates and were removed (Fig. 1). An additional 15,694 records were excluded based on the review of the study titles and abstracts, mainly because they addressed topics irrelevant to Green Prescription. Finally, we reviewed the full texts of 36 articles, of which five were not RCTs and were excluded. Ultimately, 31 articles were included in this review.

##### _3.2.1. Types of green prescription and health outcomes_ 

The green prescription regimen tested in our reviewed 31 studies varied in terms of format and outcome of the programs. The regimen format included green exercise, horticultural therapy, forest therapy, distraction therapy, and park prescription. The length of time that the interventions were undertaken ranged from one day to twelve months. The components of green prescription regimens implemented by the studies are summarized in Table 3. 

Regarding the assessed health outcomes, about three-fourths of the health outcomes were focused on psychological health and well-being (Chun et al., 2017; Diette et al., 2003; Ellisabet et al., 2015; Falk et al., 2020; Huber et al., 2019; Kerse et al., 2005; Kolt et al., 2012; Lai et al., 2018; Mao et al., 2012; Mao et al., 2017; Serrat et al., 2020; Ng et al., 2018; Palsd´ ottir et al., 2020´ ; Patel et al., 2013; Pedrinolla et al., 2019; Razani et al., 2018; Shin et al., 2012; Stigsdotter et al., 2018 Vujcic et al., 2017 Calogiuri et al., 2015; Brown et al., 2014; Yeon et al., 2022; Souter-Brown et al., 2021; Zeng et al., 2020 ~~-~~ <u>) (i.e. depression,</u> anxiety, stress, self-esteem, loneliness, happiness, psychopathology, psychosocial wellbeing, and quality of life), physical activity (Falk et al., 2020; Huber et al., 2019; Kerse et al., 2005; Kolt et al., 2012; Patel et al., 2013; Pfeiffer et al., 2001; Razani et al., 2018; Brown et al., 2014; Wells et al., 2014), and cardiometabolic health (Falk et al., 2020; Lechtzin et al., 2010; Kolt et al., 2012; Mao et al., 2012; Mao et al., 2017; Sellman et al., 2017; Williams et al., 2017; Calogiuri et al., 2015; Brown et al., 2014) (i.e. weight, body mass index, blood pressure, blood glucose, and heart rate). ##### _3.4. Effects of green prescription on cardiometabolic health_ 

Of 31 studies included in this review, Nine(Falk et al., 2020; Lechtzin et al., 2010; Kolt et al., 2012; Mao et al., 2012; Mao et al., 2017; Sellman et al., 2017; Williams et al., 2017; Calogiuri et al., 2015; Brown et al., 2014) investigated the effects of green prescription on cardiometabolic health, including systolic and diastolic blood pressure (Falk et al., 2020; Kerse et al., 2005; Kolt et al., 2012; Mao et al., 2012; Williams et al., 2017; Calogiuri et al., 2015; Brown et al., 2014), BMI (Kolt et al., 2012), cardiovascular disease-related pathological factors (Mao et al., 2012, 2017), biomarkers of heart failure (Mao et al., 2017), reduction in weight (Sellman et al., 2017), and heart rate (Williams et al., 2017; Brown et al., 2014). Five out of these nine studies reported a significant positive impact on cardiometabolic health, and four studies (Falk et al., 2020; Kerse et al., 2005; Calogiuri et al., 2015; Brown et al., 2014) reported no significant improvement after the intervention. Six studies prescribed green exercise (Kerse et al., 2005; Kolt et al., 2012; Sellman et al., 2017; Williams et al., 2017; Calogiuri et al., 2015; Brown et al., 2014), of which three reported significant improvement; Two studies prescribed forest therapy (Mao et al., 2012, 2017), and both reported significant improvements; The only study that implemented park prescription reported no significant improvement in the cardiometabolic health of the study participants. 

##### _3.5. Effects of green prescription on physical activity_ 

The effect of green prescription on physical activity was assessed as primary or secondary outcomes in nine studies (Falk et al., 2020; Huber et al., 2019; Kerse et al., 2005; Kolt et al., 2012; Patel et al., 2013; 

Pfeiffer et al., 2001; Razani et al., 2018; Brown et al., 2014; Wells et al., 2014). Among these studies, three utilized objective measures of physical activity, including accelerometers and pedometers, to provide quantitative data on participants’ physical activity levels. These objective measures allowed for an assessment of the duration, intensity, and frequency of physical activity. The remaining six studies relied on self-reporting methods, where participants provided information about their physical activity levels through surveys or questionnaires. Eight out of these studies reported a significant positive impact of green prescription on physical activity, and only one study (Huber et al., 2019) reported no significant impact. Of these studies, one prescribed horticultural therapy, two prescribed park prescriptions, while the remaining six prescribed green exercise. In five (Kerse et al., 2005; Kolt et al., 2012 Patel et al., 2013; Pfeiffer et al., 2001; Razani et al., 2018)out of the eight studies that reported a significant positive impact, both the intervention and control group received a green prescription, and the differences in individual prescription vs. group prescription, written prescription vs. verbal prescription, and green prescription vs. green prescription plus pedometer were evaluated. These studies all reported significant positive changes from baseline to follow-up, but no significant differences between groups were observed. 

##### _3.6. Other health effects of green prescription_ 

**Relieving pain:** Four studies (Diette et al., 2003; Huber et al., 2019 Lechtzin et al., 2010; Serrat et al., 2020)investigated the effects of green prescription on relieving pain. These studies employed two methods of green prescription: distraction therapy (Diette et al., 2003; Lechtzin et al., 2010) and green exercise (Huber et al., 2019; Serrat et al., 2020). In two (Diette et al., 2003; Serrat et al., 2020) of the four studies, a significant positive impact on pain control was reported. These two studies had prescribed green exercise and distraction therapy to participants undergoing flexible bronchoscopy (Diette et al., 2003) and fibromyalgia, respectively (Serrat et al., 2020). In the other two studies, no significant positive impact on pain control was reported when participants undergoing bone marrow aspiration and biopsy (Lechtzin et al., 2010) and chronic low back pain (Huber et al., 2019) received distraction therapy and green exercise, respectively. 

**Orthopedic outcomes:** Two studies (Huber et al., 2019; Kolt et al., 2012) prescribed green exercise and assessed its impact on falls, functional spine mobility, and physical function status (i.e., gait speed, chair stands, and tandem balance tests). Both studies reported no significant impact on the health outcomes measured. 

**Inflammation:** Two studies (Glenn et al., 2021; Ng et al., 2018) prescribed horticultural therapy to community-dwelling adults and observed significant changes in the biomarkers of inflammation (IL-6, IL-1β, CXCL12, RANTES, and CRP) of the intervention group, but not the control group. The prescribed horticultural therapy was associated with a reduction in the levels of plasma biomarkers that measure the extent of T-cell exhaustion and inflammation in older adults. 

**Exhaustion disorder:** Elisabet et al. prescribed forest therapy to improve the symptoms in exhaustion disorder patients but did not observe any significant improvement in the intervention group compared to the control group in terms of recovery from exhaustion disorder. 

**Social cohesion:** Lai et al. assessed the effects of horticultural therapy on social engagement in frail and prefrail nursing home residents but observed no significant improvement in the intervention group compared to the control group. 

**Use of healthcare system:** Corazon et al. assessed the effect of horticultural therapy on healthcare system use (number of contacts with a General Practitioner) and observed reduced use post-intervention. However, the control group that received cognitive behavioral therapy also showed reduced healthcare system use. The intervention and control groups were not compared, and therefore it was not possible to establish a significant difference between them.

##### _3.7. Risk of bias in included studies_ 

The summary description of the assessment of the risk of bias in individual studies is presented in Fig. 2. The majority of studies were deemed to have a low risk of bias due to inappropriate randomization, random sequence generation, allocation concealment, or baseline imbalances. There were generally some concerns due to deviations from intended interventions, as all the studies met all the items in the checklist except the criterion of blinding the treatment allocation to participants/researchers. As most outcome measures were self-reported, approximately half of the studies had some potential bias in the measurement of the outcome. This is because participants were aware of their allocation and may have anticipated receiving benefits from the intervention, which may have affected outcome measurement. None of the studies were rated as having a low overall risk of bias. Three studies had a high overall risk of bias, while the remaining twenty-eight studies were rated as having some concerns (Fig. 3). 

##### _3.8. Sources of heterogeneity_ 

There were notable variations in sample sizes and characteristics across studies. Participants were drawn from a wide range of sources, such as welfare centers, rehabilitation centers, nursing homes, workplaces, hospitals, and the community. These various populations would be expected to be associated with different baseline risks, which, in turn, might have influenced their response to the prescription. In addition to the variation in the health outcome(s) covered by our reviewed studies, there was also heterogeneity in the measurement tools used to assess health outcomes. While some studies applied subjective characterization 

of outcomes such as questionnaire-based data on mental health (Diette et al., 2003; Ellisabet et al., 2015; Falk et al., 2020; Huber et al., 2019 Kerse et al., 2005; Kolt et al., 2012; Lai et al., 2018; Mao et al., 2012 Mao et al., 2017; Serrat et al., 2020; Palsd´ ottir et al., 2020´ ; Patel et al., 2013; Shin et al., 2012; Stigsdotter et al., 2018; Vujcic et al., 2017) or physical activity (Huber et al., 2019; Kerse et al., 2005; Kolt et al., 2012 Patel et al., 2013; Pfeiffer et al., 2001) others used objective measures of outcomes such as accelerometers (Falk et al., 2020; Razani et al., 2018 Wells et al., 2014) or biomarkers (Chun et al., 2017; Ng et al., 2018 Pedrinolla et al., 2019; Razani et al., 2018). The duration of intervention was another source of variation across studies, ranging from 1 day to 12 months. For studies that compared individual green prescription to group green prescription or verbal green prescription to green written prescription, it is worth mentioning that the prescription methods did not show a notable difference between groups after the intervention. This observation might suggest that green prescription could be effective irrespective of how it was prescribed. 

##### _3.9. Current limitations and future directions_ 

The studies included in this review were mainly from high-income countries in Asia, Europe, North America, and Oceania. Studies assessing the health benefits of green prescriptions in low- and middle-income countries, particularly countries in Africa are limited. 

The lack of a control group that received no green prescription was a shortcoming in several of the studies included in this review. This implies that there could have been too much similarity between the groups to similar to detect a between-group difference in outcomes over time. Hence, it is difficult to determine if the improvements in both groups were due to participation in the trial, a natural progression of symptoms, other healthcare measures, or changes in the wider community. Future studies utilizing a control group that receives no intervention will be required to fully address the question that green prescription significantly improves reduced healthcare consumption. 

Another limitation of the RCT studies identified in this review was the lack of robust assessment of the health outcomes, particularly for psychological health, well-being, and physical activity. The majority of the studies rely on the self-reported assessment of these outcomes. This may cause an underestimation or overestimation of the effect of the intervention unless studies combine self-reported outcomes with more robust methods. Future studies should consider combining these selfreported measures with other sources of data such as doctor-diagnosed conditions (e.g., mental health status), objective measures of outcomes (e.g., physical activity, blood pressure, and heart rate variability), and biomarkers (e.g., levels of stress hormones such as cortisol). ##### **4. Conclusions** 

Our systematic review identified 31 studies evaluating the effectiveness of a range of green prescriptions on several health outcomes in a variety of settings. The quality of these studies was generally acceptable, with 28 studies having some concerns regarding their overall risk of bias and only three studies with a high risk of bias. Of these 31 studies, 16 reported a beneficial effect of green prescriptions on psychological health and well-being, eight reported a beneficial effect on physical activity, five reported a beneficial effect on cardiometabolic health, and two reported a beneficial effect on inflammation. These findings could suggest that green prescription as a public health intervention could be an important tool for improving health and well-being in the general population and supplement the conventional medicine, while enhancing the sustainability of our healthcare systems in the face of ongoing changes in the global climate. Green prescriptions are complex interventions that surpass simple advice or recommendations. They require careful consideration of various aspects, including delivery methods, adherence measurement, and the provision of additional coaching and support. By recognizing and addressing these complexities, we can unlock the full potential of green prescriptions to promote health and well-being through nature-based interventions.
